# Topic Virality Test

*Run on the question the client picked. Answers one thing: is this worth writing?*

---

---

## What this step does NOT do — hard rules

These are not preferences. Breaking any of them means the step was run wrong.

1. **No metadata.** No awareness level, no sub-dream number, no territory, no tags. The client does not need the filing system read back to them.
2. **No gap analysis.** What nobody is saying belongs in step 4.
3. **No content ideas, takes, or angles.** Those are step 3b. Producing them here contaminates the judgement — you end up scoring the idea you just invented instead of the question the client picked.
4. **No numeric score.** Not out of 15, not out of 100. Numbers imply a precision that doesn't exist here.
5. **No hedging into all-green.** If every verdict comes back green every time, the test isn't running. The criticism pass exists to find the weakness — if it finds nothing, it wasn't real criticism.
6. **The six points go in a table. Never in prose.** Four columns — Point · Assume · Attack · Land — one row per point. Paragraphs are unreadable at a glance and hide weak reasoning inside sentences. The table is not a formatting preference; it is the format.
7. **Keep the cells short.** One or two sentences each. If a cell needs a paragraph, the thought isn't finished yet.

---

## The method — assume, then attack

For each of the six points below, do three things and put all three in the table:

| Column | What goes in it |
|---|---|
| **Assume** | Your first instinct on seeing the question against this point. Plainly. |
| **Attack** | Argue against your own assumption as hard as you can. What would make it wrong? A soft attack produces a false green. |
| **Land** | What's actually true once the assumption has taken the hit. The only part that counts. |

The back-and-forth is the product. A verdict without visible reasoning is a guess with confidence added.

---

## The six points

**1. Longevity** — Will this still land in eighteen months, or is it tied to a moment?

**2. Momentum** — Are more people caring about this right now, or fewer? Rising beats flat.

**3. Demand** — How many of this audience are genuinely asking this? Not "would find it interesting" — asking.

**4. Intent** — How badly do they want it answered? Actively hunting, or idly curious?

**5. Advantage** — Do we have something on this that the existing content doesn't? Heavy competition is proof of demand, not a problem — but a crowded topic where our take *is* the consensus take is a dead post. Be brutal here: if the thing we'd say is the thing everyone already says, say so out loud.

**6. Freshness** — Will this read as new, or as something they've already scrolled past? One of three must be true: a new discovery, a new way of thinking, or a deliberate revival of something that worked.

---

## The three verdicts

Everything above resolves into exactly three answers. Nothing else is reported.

| | The question | What it's really testing |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Will this get a lot of views?** | Is the room big, and can we win attention inside it |
| **2** | **Will this attract the right people?** | Is the person asking this the person who could buy |
| **3** | **Will solving this mean something to them?** | If they get a real answer, does their situation change |

Each gets a colour. No number, no percentage.

- 🟢 **Green** — yes, and the reasoning survived the attack
- 🟡 **Yellow** — conditional. **State the condition that flips it green.** A yellow without a named condition is useless.
- 🔴 **Red** — no

---

## The gate

- **Any red — do not write it.** Fix the specific thing, or go back and let the client pick another question.
- **Views must reach green.** Yellow on views means the room is too small or too crowded to win. It can still be written *if* the named condition is met and the client accepts it.
- **Two yellows is a warning. Three is a different question.**
- **All three green — write it.**
