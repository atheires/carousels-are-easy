# Intake

*Run once per client. Nothing is written to disk until it's finished.*

---

**One question at a time. Never a question paper.**

Ask a question. Wait. Read what comes back. React to it — reflect it, sharpen it, or push on the vague part — and only then ask the next one. If the client already knows exactly what they're doing, take their answer and move on. If they're fumbling, help them find it: offer shapes, give examples, think out loud with them. This is a conversation between two people working something out, not a form being filled.

Nothing is written to disk until the whole intake is done.

There are **four questions**, then **two things you build and show them**.

---

**Q1 — Who are you helping?**

Not a demographic. A person. Push until you can picture one.

"Entrepreneurs" is not an answer. "People who quit their job six months ago and haven't replaced the income yet" is. If they give you a category, ask what that person's week actually looks like, or what they'd say if you asked them how it's going.

**Q2 — What are they trying to get?**

The outcome they're chasing, in their words. This is the anchor for everything downstream — the playground is built from it, the Dream Ladder in step 2 is built from it, and every topic has to plausibly affect it.

**Q3 — What do you sell them?**

The actual thing. Name, price, and format. Price matters because it sets how hot a lead has to be before the ask makes sense — a $27 community can convert a curious commenter, a $3k container cannot.

If nothing exists yet, that's a real answer. Say so plainly and keep going; it becomes a flagged gap, not a blocker.

**Q4 — How do you help them get it?**

The mechanism. What you actually believe the route is, and what you teach. Not the product's features — the position.

This one decides what every carousel is allowed to terminate at. If the mechanism is "clients come to you through content," then a carousel that ends by teaching a better cold DM has recruited the right person and sold them something adjacent. It fails, no matter how well it reads.

---

Then you build two things and put them in front of the client.

**Build 1 — The Playground**

From Q1, Q2 and Q4, map every territory that legitimately affects whether this person gets that outcome. This replaces the niche. It is the entire surface you're allowed to write about, and it should feel almost uncomfortably wide.

Start from these standing territories and keep whichever genuinely bear on the outcome:

- **The mechanism itself** — the thing they're actually here to learn
- **Productivity and time** — how the work gets done at all
- **AI and tools** — what's changing about the work right now
- **Business models** — the different shapes the outcome can take
- **Competitors and the industry** — what everyone else in this space is selling and where they're wrong
- **Books and thinkers** — borrowed credibility and borrowed frameworks
- **The client's own story** — the problems they hit and how they got out
- **Mentors** — who taught them what, and what it cost
- **Money** — pricing, spending, what it's for
- **Mindset and psychology** — why they don't do the thing they know to do
- **Health, energy, environment** — the physical conditions the outcome depends on
- **Relationships** — the people around them who make it easier or harder

Then add the ones specific to this client that the list doesn't cover.

**A territory earns its place if you can finish this sentence:** *"This affects whether they get [the outcome] because ___."* If you can't, cut it.

Show the playground. Ask what's missing and what they'd refuse to touch.

**Build 2 — The Founding Beliefs**

Do not ask the client what they'd never say. At the start they don't know, and asking produces a shrug.

Build it instead. Take what this audience commonly believes — the received wisdom, the advice they've absorbed, the things they'd say without thinking — and **flip each one with logic.** Not contrarianism for its own sake. Each flip needs reasoning underneath it good enough that a reader thinks *someone actually thought about this,* not *someone is being difficult.*

Aim for **5–8 flipped beliefs.** Each one written as:

> **They believe:** ___
> **The flip:** ___
> **Why the flip holds:** ___ *(the actual reasoning — a mechanism, a consequence, a case where the common belief visibly fails)*

These become the client's standing positions. Every carousel should be traceable to at least one. They're also the fastest route to a strong Perspective score in step 5.

Show them. The client will react to a position far faster than they'd generate one — that reaction is the real intake. Change whatever they push back on, and keep changing them as they sharpen over time.

---

**Then write `Clients/[name]/About Client.md`:**

The person · the outcome · the product · the mechanism · the Playground · the Founding Beliefs · voice notes · durable feedback.

**No goal field.** The goal is fixed system-wide — views, then leads. **No CTA field.** Comment words are decided per-carousel in step 8, and only a log of already-used words is kept so they don't get recycled.
