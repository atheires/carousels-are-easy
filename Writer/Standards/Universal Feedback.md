# Universal Feedback

**Every craft law learned from every client. Applies to all of them, always.**

Read before writing anything. Run the gate at the bottom before delivering anything.

Client folders hold only what is true of *that* client. If a rule would improve any client's work, it belongs here instead.

**Feedback stacks. Nothing here is ever replaced, only added to.** Fixing the newest note while quietly dropping an older one is itself the failure, and it is the most repeated failure on record.

---

## A. Truth

**A1. Never invent client facts.** No fabricated metrics, testimonials, stories or results. Write `[OPERATOR: insert real story/number here]` and flag it in chat.

**A2. Slide 1 headlines are the one exception, and only because clients authorised it.** Write a real-shaped number straight in, never a placeholder, because a headline with a hole in it cannot be judged and a working structure gets rejected for the wrong reason. The client fixes digits in post.

**A3. Use their words verbatim when they tell you what they said.** A tighter rewrite of a pitch that never happened is a fabrication wearing better clothes.

**A4. Never claim to know what strangers thought, saw or did.** "Every freelancer scrolled past that post" is unknowable, and it reads as arrogance.

**A5. Every big claim gets backed.** A number, a named book, a named person, real proof, an analogy that genuinely bridges, a logical reason, or open humour. A claim followed by another claim is not backing.

---

## B. The question bank

**B1. Say the subject, never encode it.** "Clients who actually have a budget" is a dressed-up way of writing "good clients." If the subject is saturation, the word *saturated* appears.

**B2. Short.** Five to ten words. Past twelve, something is hiding in a clause.

**B3. No cleverness, no hindsight framing, no sugarcoating.** Would they type it into Google or send it in a DM? If it only works as a headline, it is a topic pretending to be a question.

**B4. Never generate for volume.** Fifty real questions beat a hundred where sixty are rephrasings. Fewer is explicitly fine.

**B5. Awareness levels stay clean inside a block.** A scaling question does not sit in a getting-started section.

**B6. Harvest three kinds.** Concerns, dream-state, and limiting beliefs. A file of only pains produces only complaint posts.

**B7. Ground it in real language before writing.** Copy their phrasing, do not improve it.

## D. Writing the carousel

**D1. The question chain is the structure.** Every slide creates a question and the next slide answers it. If a slide answers a question nobody asked, it is filler.

**D2. Slide 2 pays the debt slide 1 left.** Work out which of New, Easy, Safe and Big the headline already spent, then deliver the missing ones. After a big claim the reader is skeptical, not curious.

**D3. No two neighbouring slides share a shape.** Rotate: single line, mini paragraph, bullets, quote, long slide, two-beat, short slide. Every carousel needs at least one single-line slide, one paragraph and one list.

**D4. Write it the way it would be said out loud.** Flowing sentences with commas in them. Fewer full stops. No three-word fragment punchlines. If it sounds like an advert it is wrong.

**D5. The belief-shifting line gets a slide to itself.** Burying the best line inside a paragraph is the most common way to waste it.

**D6. Stay humble.** No superiority tax. Credit luck where luck happened. Keep the reader on your side of the table.

**D8. Slide 2 buys trust, and it does not move.** Proof, a named credible source, the short story behind the conclusion, or visible effort. Not slide 4.

**D9. Precision about them beats borrowed authority.** Never open on a corporate case study. Describe their situation more precisely than they would themselves.

**D10. The assumption beat is present, in their real practical words.** Say what they already think is coming, then refuse it. Never a therapy label.

**D11. Validate before you pivot.** Agree with the decision they made and name where the belief came from. Then move them. Never make them feel stupid for what they did.

**D12. Their objection appears in quote marks and gets answered.**

**D13. Insight lands as a scene or analogy, never an abstract noun.**

**D14. Show the upgrade, do not describe it.** Raw input, what they would normally write, what it becomes. Then a second example so it transfers.

**D15. Real value, not only reasons.** "Never give the how" is not permission to give nothing. Teach enough for a rough version tonight, and hold back the genuinely hard-alone part for the comment word.

**D16. Vary the texture.** Alternate dense, light, single line, comparison, bullets. Sameness is what makes a post feel machine-made.

**D17. Personality over polish.** Be a bit unprofessional. A specific jab beats a polished sentence. At least one laugh, and never aimed at the reader.

**D18. Every detail they supplied is either used or deliberately cut.** Dropping their detail to keep slides short is the wrong trade. Detail is the difference between a story and a summary.

**D19. The close turns into the ask.** One ask, never two. The comment word is the obvious next move, not a bolt-on, and it routes into doing rather than knowing more.

**D20. Contractions, plain words, short sentences.** If a line sounds like a consultant wrote it, cut it.

**D21. No em dashes. No semicolons. No "not just X but Y". No hedges.** Voicing the reader's own objection as a question is allowed and wanted.

## Generic advice

If anyone could have given the advice, it does not belong in the carousel. Every tip must come from the author's own build or result and give the reader a way to save work, time or money, not general craft tips. Source: author feedback, 11 October 2026, "It feels generic and I feel like this advice can be given by anyone."
