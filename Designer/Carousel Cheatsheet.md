## PART 1 — THE 10 LAWS OF "STAY"

These are the patterns that appear in EVERY Stay version and are ABSENT from every Skip version.

### LAW 1 — The hook must promise an **outcome, emotion, or secret** — never describe the photo.

**What this law means:** Your headline should not simply state what the viewer is looking at. Instead, it should create curiosity or desire by promising a benefit, a feeling, or insider knowledge. Descriptive hooks ("Outfit of the day") tell the brain there's nothing new to discover—the image already says it all. Promising hooks ("The Art of Layering that turns outfits into statements") make the brain want to swipe to find out HOW or WHY. This hooks into psychology: we stop scrolling for things we want to learn, feel, or experience—not for descriptions of what's in front of us.

### LAW 2 — Title hierarchy: ONE huge phrase + ONE small subtitle. Never a single flat line.

**What this law means:** A two-tier title structure works because it creates visual rhythm and allows the headline to do two jobs. The big word grabs attention (the "5-second stop"), and the small subline provides context or emotion (the "stay to read" moment). This hierarchy signals that the design is intentional—not a quick phone screenshot. A flat single-line headline forces all the meaning into one font size, making it feel generic or desperate. The contrast between sizes creates visual interest and makes the cover feel more like professional editorial content than an Instagram Story.

### LAW 3 — A **magazine-style serif** is the Stay font. Generic handwritten / italic script = Skip.

**What this law means:** Font choice is a signal of intentionality. Bold serifs (Playfair, Bodoni, DM Serif) and modern condensed sans-serifs communicate "this was designed by someone who knows what they're doing." Generic yellow italic script on a raw photo sends the opposite signal: "I grabbed my phone and added text." Typography works subconsciously—viewers don't think "oh that's Bodoni," but their brain registers "this looks professional/edited." Serif fonts feel editorial and high-status; they tell the story you're crafting, not describing.

### LAW 4 — Stay covers use a **frame, card, polaroid, journal, postcard, or magazine layout** — never the raw photo alone.

**What this law means:** A raw full-bleed photo with text looks like a screenshot—it reads as casual and unedited. When you put the image inside a container (a polaroid, journal page, magazine frame, postcard, grid, etc.), it immediately signals "this is curated content." The frame does psychological work: it says "I chose to show you this in this way." Frames also create visual interest by breaking up the monotony of a single large image. The container becomes part of the story—a polaroid says "memory," a journal says "personal," a grid says "collection," a magazine says "editorial expertise."

### LAW 5 — Add 2–4 **visual supporting elements** around the hero subject.

**What this law means:** Supporting elements (stickers, labels, arrows, color dots, cutouts) prevent the eye from leaving the slide quickly. When a cover feels sparse, the viewer scrolls. When it's populated with intentional details, the viewer dwells. These elements also communicate depth: a dress with labeled details (price, brand, materials) teases the carousel content. Surrounding icons or cutouts prove that there's more to discover if you swipe. The visual clutter (when done intentionally) paradoxically creates more interest than a clean, empty slide. It feels like a mood board or curated collection rather than a single product shot.

### LAW 6 — Background **darkens, contrasts, or simplifies** behind the text.

**What this law means:** Text placed on a busy or bright area becomes invisible or hard to read. Stay covers solve this by either darkening the photo area behind text, using a solid colored background to place the image on, adding an overlay strip, or replacing the background entirely with a texture that contrasts with the headline. This is about readability AND design hierarchy: the background should support the text, not compete with it. When text fights a busy photo, it signals amateur design. When text sits cleanly on a flat zone, it feels intentional and professional.

### LAW 7 — **Cutout the subject** and place on a designed background.

**What this law means:** When you cut a subject (a person, outfit, food, product) out of its original photo and place it on a designed background, you immediately signal editorial/design work. Full uncropped photos feel like raw life—candid snapshots. Cutouts feel intentional and curated. Cutouts also allow you to control the context (what background, what color story, what mood) independent of the original photo. This gives you compositional control and makes the cover feel like part of a unified design system rather than a random photo with text slapped on it.

### LAW 8 — Use a **visual format people already love**: before/after, swatch card, postcard, phone-in-frame, polaroid, grid, journal page, magazine spread.

**What this law means:** Your brain already knows what a before/after means (transformation), what a polaroid means (memory), what a magazine grid means (curated selection). When you borrow these formats, you leverage existing psychological shortcuts. The viewer's brain instantly recognizes the format and feels comfortable—there's no friction. A format your audience has never seen requires them to decode what they're looking at first, which creates friction. Formats like before/after, polaroid, postcard, grid, and journal are psychological shortcuts that tell a story without you having to explain it.

### LAW 9 — **Emotion / storytelling > facts.**

**What this law means:** Facts alone (ingredients, specifications, price) don't stop the scroll. Emotion does. When you frame something through a feeling or story, you create identification. "5 skincare steps" is forgettable; "POV: cleared my acne through food" makes people stop because they either relate to the pain or want to learn the secret. Stories and emotions activate mirror neurons—your audience doesn't just read your words, they FEEL them. A jacket with specs is a product; a jacket that "turns outfits into statements" is a transformation. Storytelling moves the focus from the THING to the PERSON using the thing, which is always more compelling.

### LAW 10 — Color words / bolded keywords inside the subtitle.

**What this law means:** Bolding one or two keywords creates a second moment of interest within the subtitle. Your eye lands on the big headline, then drops to the small subtitle—and the bolded word becomes a third hook. Bolding works because it creates visual rhythm and helps readers scan (which is what everyone does—they scan before they read). The bolded word should be the emotional punch or the surprising word. "Jewelry you won't stop thinking about" with "won't stop" bolded is more memorable than the same sentence without emphasis. Bolding = intentional typography that guides the eye and amplifies the most important concept.

## PART 3 — THE STAY TEMPLATES (steal these structures)

When the user gives me an idea, I match it to one of these and write the cover:

1. **MAGAZINE COVER** — Giant serif headline + tiny italic subtitle + framed product. Use for: any "details / picks / edit" content.
2. **POSTCARD / POLAROID / JOURNAL** — Borrowed-format frame around the photo, handwritten subline + stamp/clip/tape sticker. Use for: travel, lifestyle, OOTD, intimate behind-the-scenes.
3. **FLAT-LAY MOOD BOARD** — Subject cut out on solid bg + 3–6 sticker cutouts of associated items, hand-drawn arrows w/ labels. Use for: outfits, routines, "what's in my…", pet content, ingredients.
4. **BEFORE / AFTER SPLIT** — Same frame split vertically or horizontally, problem half desaturated, solution half warm/lit. Use for: skincare, fitness, interior, hair, transformation of any kind.
5. **3×3 PROCESS GRID** — 9 thumbnails teasing every step. Use for: routines, recipes, restaurants, tutorials. Promise: "you'll see the WHOLE thing if you swipe."
6. **TORN-PAPER / WINDOW CUTOUT** — Cream textured card with rectangular windows revealing the pain point (acne close-up, before-state, problem zoom). Use for: clinics, services that fix a visible problem.
7. **MEME / RELATABILITY DUAL-PANEL** — "X as a kid: [icon]. X as an adult: [icons]." Use for: lifestyle products, fashion, jewelry — anywhere identity humor lands.
8. **PHONE-IN-PHOTO** — A mockup phone screen showing the human/customer moment overlaid on the product photo. Use for: restaurants, brands, "in the wild" testimonials.
9. **STORYTELLING TWO-SHOT** — Day one / One day. Then / now. Year 1 / Year 30. Use for: heirloom products, brands with longevity, emotional categories.
10. **HEADLINE STRADDLE** — One serif word above the image, one serif word below ("CAPTURING / MAGIC PIECES"). Use for: product shots that are already strong — let scale and framing do the work.

## PART 4 — THE HOOK-REWRITE FORMULAS

Whenever a user gives me a Skip-style hook, I run it through these:

- **Descriptive → Promise:** "My skincare routine" → "The routine that *actually* cleared my skin in 21 days"
- **Generic → Specific:** "5 travel tips" → "5 toxic itinerary habits that ruined my last vacation"
- **Feature → Feeling:** "Highlights of Mendoza" → "Moments from Mendoza that altered my brain chemistry"
- **Tip → Secret:** "3 things I learned" → "3 *unwritten rules* of…"
- **Item → Memory:** "Jewelry you should gift" → "You remember that summer fling?"
- **Statement → POV:** "I cleared my acne" → "POV: cleared my acne through food"
- **Product → Identity:** "Pieces you can wear" → "Capturing magic pieces"
- **Question → Tease:** "What's a good routine?" → "The routine you didn't know you needed"
- **Boring noun → Loaded adjective:** add VIRAL, TOXIC, UNWRITTEN, FORBIDDEN, RUINED, MAGIC, ULTIMATE, BRAIN-CHEMISTRY-ALTERING, NEVER-STOP-TALKING-ABOUT

Punchwords to keep in rotation: **viral, unwritten, toxic, ruined, altered, magic, POV, ultimate, can't stop, won't stop, never stop, forbidden, secret, lies, art of, rules of, anatomy of**.

## PART 5 — INSTANT SKIP CHECKLIST (red flags I will refuse to ship)

If my cover has any of these, it's a Skip and I must redesign:
- [ ] Yellow handwritten/italic script on a raw phone photo
- [ ] One flat title that DESCRIBES the photo ("Outfit of the day", "My routine")
- [ ] Single font size, no hierarchy
- [ ] No frame / no container / no cutout — just full-bleed photo
- [ ] No supporting visual elements (no stickers/labels/arrows/dots)
- [ ] Text floats on a busy, bright, low-contrast area
- [ ] Hook describes WHAT instead of teasing WHY
- [ ] No emotion, no story, no identity claim, no number, no insider word
- [ ] Looks like an Instagram Story screenshot, not a designed slide
