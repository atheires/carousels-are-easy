# Design principles reference

Use before designing and when inspecting the actual rendered image. A principle is a tool for solving a communication problem; the goal is not to use every technique on every slide. This reference combines the four supplied video lessons with the user's explicit preferences. The two-line ban is a user rule, not a rule claimed by the videos.

## Mandatory cover rule

**Never use a two-line cover headline. Never make a cover whose complete visible text consists of exactly two lines.** Use one line when it fits at a readable size, or three or more purposeful lines. Do not add filler, shrink the headline to force one line, or add a tiny label just to evade the rule. Do not break one primary headline into two separate one-line objects as a workaround. Supporting copy and labels remain subordinate; three lines must not become three equally loud headlines. Preserve exact approved wording, case, punctuation and order. Reflow is allowed; rewriting requires authorization.

## Start with the brief

Record: audience; slide's communication goal; exact copy; format and actual viewing size; approved brand/reference qualities; must-keep objects; intended mood; the one primary entry point; secondary explanation; finishing information/action. Decide which techniques help those jobs and which do not. A sparse cover, dense teaching slide and CTA have different requirements.

## 1. Hierarchy and focal point

**Meaning:** establish degrees of importance. Scale, weight, contrast, isolation, position and lighting all contribute. The visually strongest hook may be an image; practical information need not be the largest element.

**Check:** name what appears dominant in the rendered image, then the next two groups. Does this match the brief? Inspect at full size and a phone-size preview. Can the hook still be understood without reading every small detail?

**Failure evidence:** every line has display scale; a decorative word overwhelms the meaningful phrase; a face, label or colored block competes with the intended hook; supporting copy disappears.

**Repair:** reduce the competing element's scale/weight/contrast, regroup the hook, or isolate the intended focal point. Enlarge only when needed. Specify the exact element and relative adjustment. Do not prescribe a giant headline as the universal fix.

## 2. Proximity and semantic grouping

**Meaning:** related things should look like a group. Their internal gaps should generally be smaller than gaps separating unrelated groups.

**Check:** can a reader tell which caption belongs to which object, which lines form one thought, and which details form a unit? Are comparison labels near their respective subjects?

**Failure evidence:** a caption sits closer to the wrong picture; a connector floats away from its sentence; unrelated sections look fused; event details are scattered.

**Repair:** move the caption toward its subject, close internal spacing, or widen the boundary between groups. Adjust distances before adding boxes or dividers.

## 3. Alignment, continuity and optical correction

**Meaning:** shared edges, baselines and directions make placements feel connected. Optical alignment accounts for curved letters, punctuation, irregular cutouts and uneven visual mass.

**Check:** identify the common edge or baseline of comparable elements. Look for accidental almost-alignments. Check actual rendered text, not only coordinates in a prompt.

**Failure evidence:** similar labels begin at slightly different edges; equal-role captions sit at inconsistent heights; an object and associated text have no clear relationship; a mathematically centered shape looks off-center.

**Repair:** choose one useful common edge/baseline, align the relevant objects to it, then make small optical corrections. Intentional offsets need a compositional purpose; not everything must be left-aligned or centered.

## 4. Similarity and repetition

**Meaning:** consistent visual treatment tells readers that elements have the same role. Repeat meaningful type roles, colors, icon styles, margins and spacing, rather than repeating every scene.

**Check:** do equivalent labels and steps share treatment? Across a carousel, do the type roles and brand remain recognizable while examples vary appropriately?

**Failure evidence:** identical roles use arbitrary fonts/colors; icons have unrelated stroke weights; the deck changes its margin logic; every slide repeats the same picture without adding meaning.

**Repair:** define reusable roles and correct the outlier. Allow different treatment when the meaning or approved account style calls for it.

## 5. Macro negative space

**Meaning:** large empty areas separate groups, isolate a focal point and affect mood and pace. Empty space need not be white. More emptiness does not automatically improve a design.

**Check:** identify what each major open region does: separates, frames, balances, pauses, or communicates absence/quiet. Does it support the intended mood? Are important elements still readable?

**Failure evidence:** everything is crowded; one huge vacant quadrant is disconnected from the composition; the subject is so small that the message loses impact; open space is filled with irrelevant decoration.

**Repair:** redistribute groups, reserve space around the anchor, or enlarge/reposition a weak subject. Avoid arbitrary percentage targets and random blank areas.

## 6. Micro space and typography

**Meaning:** letter spacing, word spacing, line spacing and local padding determine readability and grouping. A headline should read as a coherent thought, not a collection of styled fragments.

**Check:** kerning, counters, leading, semantic line breaks, punctuation, typeface/weight roles and text-object clearance. Read the exact words from the output. Verify the cover line rule above.

**Failure evidence:** cramped or excessively loose leading; disconnected sentence fragments; tiny connector words; an isolated huge keyword; awkward word breaks; inconsistent spacing; illegible thin serif; clipped letters.

**Repair:** reflow at meaningful phrase boundaries, correct leading/kerning, simplify type treatments and reserve readable space. Use one dominant treatment plus genuinely subordinate roles. Never rewrite or omit copy to fit without authorization.

## 7. Grid and structure

**Meaning:** a grid coordinates placement, hierarchy, line length and reserved space. Use the simplest grid suited to the content.

**Check:** can you identify the column/margin logic? Do the number of columns spanned and the space allocated reflect importance? Are deliberate breaks distinguishable from mistakes?

**Options:** column/asymmetric/hierarchical grids for posters; baseline grids for related text; modular/square grids for repeated steps or galleries; manuscript grids for sustained reading; thirds for initial subject placement; compound grids only when needed. Circular, triangular and isometric grids serve particular construction tasks. Golden ratio is optional, not a quality guarantee.

**Failure evidence:** arbitrary text positions, inconsistent gutters, uncomfortable line lengths, or a grid that forces all content into equal importance.

**Repair:** select a useful grid, reserve empty areas and establish shared edges. Permit one purposeful break if it improves the message. Do not overlay multiple grids to make a simple slide seem sophisticated.

## 8. Balance and visual weight

**Meaning:** size, darkness, saturation, detail and isolation affect perceived weight. Asymmetric balance can feel stable without mirrored placement.

**Check:** compare left/right and top/bottom masses, including the influence of empty space. Does the layout feel intentionally weighted or accidentally tipped?

**Failure evidence:** a heavy object pulls everything into one corner; a large headline and object overload the same area; a tiny decorative object is mistaken for a useful counterweight.

**Repair:** move or resize a specific mass, reduce unnecessary darkness/detail, or redistribute space. Do not add a meaningless object just to fill the opposite corner.

## 9. Movement and eye guidance

**Meaning:** hierarchy, alignment, curves, gaze and directional shapes can suggest a route. Arrows are one option. A main route may have subordinate side routes when the information warrants them.

**Check:** describe the likely entry, next group and finish from the actual image. Do captions and subjects lead toward useful information or off the canvas? Does the route preserve reading order?

**Failure evidence:** several competing entry points; eye jumps between unrelated areas; a gaze leads away from the message; a decorative direction bypasses the explanation.

**Repair:** strengthen the intended anchor, rearrange the next group along a shared edge/curve, reduce competing cues, or reorient a subject. Avoid claiming a guaranteed eye path without observation of viewers.

## 10. Contrast beyond color

**Meaning:** contrast can involve light/dark, saturation, scale, weight, typeface, shape, texture and style. Contrast of associations or emotion can communicate a deliberate unexpected idea.

**Check:** identify which difference establishes importance and which supports the concept. Verify text/background contrast at the viewing size. Is every accent competing for attention?

**Failure evidence:** everything has equal weight; text merges into its background; many accent colors; inconsistent illustration/photo styles with no purpose; a stylistic contradiction changes the intended meaning.

**Repair:** choose the smallest effective contrast change. A curved subject against straight alignments or textured object against a quiet field may solve blandness without another color or font. Use psychological/emotional contradiction only when the brief supports it.

## 11. Layering, depth and occlusion

**Meaning:** overlap, crop, scale, shadow and foreground/background relationships can give depth and integrate type with imagery. Layering is an application of the lessons' overlap, contrast and friction ideas; it is not mandatory decoration.

**Check:** is the front/back order clear? Are shadows and perspective coherent? Does overlap preserve word recognition, faces and essential subject features? Inspect tiny gaps and near-tangencies.

**Failure evidence:** a cutout floats; shadows disagree; a layer hides a required word; an edge almost touches a letter; overlapping elements look accidental; too many depth effects compete.

**Repair:** establish a clear layer order, adjust the overlapping edge or gap, ground the subject, and remove gratuitous layers. A deliberately flat design may pass without layering. Never force obscured lettering to appear dynamic.

## 12. Rhythm, pace and implied motion

**Meaning:** repetition and changing scale/density can create rhythm. Still images can suggest movement through directional forms, progressive scale, frozen action or blur. Alternating impact, detail and open space can create a viewing pace.

**Check:** does repetition unify the composition or make it monotonous? Does the pace match the mood and information density? Across slides, is visual variety useful?

**Failure evidence:** a supposedly energetic design feels static; identical frames add nothing; motion effects obscure the subject; rapid visual cues contradict a calm message.

**Repair:** use a meaningful crop, asymmetry, one scale shift or directional relationship. Add implied motion only when it helps. Do not assert measured dwell time or invent energy the brief does not need.

## 13. Friction and flow

**Meaning:** one controlled disruption can attract attention while surrounding reading remains clear. Friction may be an overlap, crop, unexpected angle or tighter area; it must have a job.

**Check:** identify the disruption and its purpose. Can the message still be read easily? Does enough consistent structure exist for the disruption to register?

**Failure evidence:** random rotated objects; multiple competing disruptions; obstructed type; generic symmetry that misses an energetic brief; every section feels equally intense.

**Repair:** remove unnecessary disruptions or introduce one specific, relevant contrast/crop. Preserve clean reading zones. Distinguish calmness from blandness using the brief, not personal preference.

## 14. Negative-space concept and figure/ground

**Meaning:** space can reveal a hidden object, use a letter counter/gap, form an interdependent foreground/background image, or imply absence. This is different from simply leaving room around a subject.

**Check:** if an illusion is intended, can both readings be recognized and do they relate to the message? Are contours continuous? Is the text still readable? Does absence communicate the intended idea?

**Failure evidence:** hidden silhouette is ambiguous or unrelated; a letter becomes unreadable; the designer calls ordinary whitespace a hidden-object technique; the second reading requires an explanation to exist.

**Repair:** refine contour continuity and simplify the competing shapes, or remove the illusion. Record this principle as intentionally omitted when the brief does not benefit from it.

## 15. Concept, interest and restraint

**Meaning:** a meaningful visual metaphor or specific scene creates interest. Decoration should support the message and chosen medium. No checklist can guarantee taste, but it can expose generic or unexplained decisions.

**Check:** explain the connection between the copy and subject. For a design called bland, identify the actual weakness: generic image, weak contrast, monotonous rhythm, ineffective crop, or missing focal point. Is texture deliberate and coherent rather than accidental generation noise?

**Failure evidence:** recycled reference subject unrelated to the new copy; generic background; random icon; more layers used as a substitute for a concept; plausible-looking but malformed objects.

**Repair:** improve the metaphor/scene or one relevant compositional device. Preserve liked objects and approved brand qualities. Do not change the whole style to solve a local spacing defect.

## 16. Transferability and production integrity

**Meaning:** the hierarchy and identity should survive intended viewing conditions. Different formats often require recomposition rather than cropping.

**Check:** full size and a small preview of approximately 320–400 pixels wide; actual requested aspect ratio; safe edges; exact text; intended slide order/count; correct final version; consistency with approved branding. Test additional formats only if actually needed.

**Failure evidence:** unreadable practical details, clipping, invented words, omitted copy, margins lost in a crop, different type roles between slides, or a false claim of universal scalability.

**Repair:** enlarge/reflow support copy, move critical content inside safe margins, repair generation defects, or compose a separate requested adaptation. No unverified cross-format claims.

## Compact pre-delivery checklist

- [ ] Exact copy, case, punctuation, order and slide count preserved.
- [ ] Cover headline is one line or at least three lines; no two-line workaround.
- [ ] Actual focal point and subordinate groups match the communication goal.
- [ ] Related copy and images group clearly; equivalent roles use consistent treatment.
- [ ] Shared edges/baselines and optical alignment are intentional.
- [ ] Macro space has a purpose; micro spacing supports reading.
- [ ] Grid and visual balance fit the content.
- [ ] A likely reading route connects the useful information.
- [ ] Contrast establishes importance without competing accents.
- [ ] Layers, crops, shadows and tangencies preserve clarity.
- [ ] Rhythm/energy match the brief; blandness claims identify a concrete cause.
- [ ] Any disruption or negative-space illusion earns its place; omissions are justified.
- [ ] Full-size and phone-size inspection completed, with limitations recorded.
- [ ] Independent critic reviewed the actual current image; required fixes verified.
