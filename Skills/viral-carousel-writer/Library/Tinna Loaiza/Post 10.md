## PART 2 — ANALYSIS

### SLIDE 1 — THE HOOK

"How to Become Consistent at (almost) Anything"

**Mechanism: a maximally broad promise, de-risked by a parenthesis.**

This is the opposite hook strategy to post 9. There is no identity gate, no age, no "I". It is a flat, literal how-to headline aimed at the single most universal complaint in self-improvement: I start things and stop.

Three things make it work:
- **The topic is not niched at all.** "Consistent at anything" recruits people who want to post, train, diet, write, or launch. Anyone who has ever quit something is in the audience.
- **"(almost)" is the whole hook.** Strip it and the headline is an over-promise a sceptical reader dismisses on sight. Keep it and it reads as honest, self-aware, slightly amused — and it *increases* the credibility of the "anything." She sets it in small italics so it reads as an aside rather than a caveat. A humility marker doing conversion work.
- **The design is the differentiator, not the copy.** A sky photograph with retro pale-yellow type, in a feed of white-and-green text slides. The words are the plainest in her whole set; the frame is the least plain.

**What curiosity does it open?** Only "what is the method?" — a mechanism gap. It does **not** hint that the answer will be counterintuitive; that surprise is held back for slide 2.

**What it hides:** the mechanism entirely, and also the *shape* of the answer. A reader arrives at slide 2 expecting habit-stacking or discipline tips.

### THE MESSAGE

**What she is arguing:** Inconsistency is not a willpower deficiency, it is a *validation-sourcing* error. You measure progress against the outcome; the outcome is uncertain; uncertainty reads as danger to the nervous system; so you withdraw. Fix consistency by moving your source of validation from the outcome (not in your control, not immediately visible) to your output (in your control, visible today).

**Reader at the start:** "I'm inconsistent because I'm undisciplined. I lack willpower. Other people just want it more than me."

**Reader at the end:** "I'm inconsistent because I keep checking the scoreboard, and the scoreboard doesn't move fast enough to keep me going. It's not a character flaw, it's a measurement problem. And I can change what I measure today, for free."

The specific persuasive move: she takes an accusation the reader makes against herself (*I'm weak*) and converts it into a **mechanical fault with a switch** (*you are pointing your dopamine at the wrong number*). Blame is removed and agency returned in the same breath.

### SLIDE-BY-SLIDE PROGRESSION

**S1 — Promise + credibility caveat.** States the outcome, quietly signals honesty with "(almost)". Hand-off: pure curiosity about the method; nothing else to hold onto.

**S2 — Enemy identification via a proverb.** She takes a phrase nobody argues with, "If there is a will there is a way," and asks whether the will is the problem: "But what if wanting it so much is the very thing stopping you from being consistent?" Hand-off device: **a question that inverts a cliché.** Quoting a proverb the reader half-believes and turning it inside out is the strongest transition in the deck, because it creates a *contradiction* rather than a gap. The reader cannot resolve it without swiping.

**S3 — Mechanism, part 1: the cause.** Names it ("over-reliance on willpower"), then explains it physiologically: "Willpower is finite and often conditioned to a validation loop… our brain boosts the feel-good signals and dopamine keeps us going." Hand-off device: **an explicit self-interrupting question.** "But validation is not always immediate. / Or is it?" — she raises a doubt about her own sentence. Three words, and a promised twist.

**S4 — Mechanism, part 2: the failure loop dramatised in second person.** The only slide with real narrative texture: "Right now you take one step. Maybe two or three if you're feeling motivated. Then you lift your head up to see how close you are to your goal and see that it's still miles away. So you withdraw." Then the diagnosis: "It's biology. Uncertainty feels unsafe and we stop pursuing the unknown as a way of self-preservation." Hand-off device: **an explicit promise of relief** — "Luckily there is a way around this." She names the solution's existence without naming the solution.

**S5 — Reframe made visual.** Goal at the top with a medal, a scattered cloud of dashes labelled "The steps." The argument: you cannot know the route, only that not stepping guarantees stasis, therefore "The only certainty is the need to move… Everything around it — the time, the conditions, even the goal itself — becomes part of the background." Hand-off: "part of the background" demotes the goal, preparing the reader to accept that the goal is not the thing to measure. No cliffhanger here; the diagram's novelty earns the swipe instead.

**S6 — The payoff.** "It compounds when you redirect your source of validation to the only constant within your control. / **Your output.**" The answer, in two words, isolated on its own highlighted line. Then it closes the title loop verbatim: "the only focus point that will make you consistent (at almost anything)". Hand-off: the payoff is abstract, so slide 7 must make it concrete — the reader swipes because "your output" is not yet usable.

**S7 — Application.** The six-row instead-of table converts the abstraction into six literal measurement swaps, then adds the social-comparison warning ("Consistency looks different when you see it in someone's Instagram highlight") and her own receipt ("I found a much healthier relationship with following through on things when I refocused my attention on the things that were actually within my control now, not the ideal scenario I allowed social media to fabricate in my mind"). The most useful slide in the deck, placed second-to-last.

**S8 — About-me + follow CTA.** No offer, no comment word. Introduces herself properly and asks for the follow, with a mechanical tip attached (add to favourites).

**Structural read:** unlike post 9's interchangeable stack, this deck is a **strict causal chain** — proverb, cause, biological mechanism, visual reframe, payoff, application. You cannot reorder slides 2 through 7 without breaking the argument. That is why it is 8 slides and not 12: a chain has a natural length, a stack does not.

### KNOWLEDGE GAPS

**Total: 5, and — unusually for her — every single one closes inside the carousel.**

**Gap 1 — "What is the method?"**
- Opens: S1 (the how-to headline). Withheld: the entire mechanism.
- Closes: S6 ("Your output."). **Full reveal.** Held open across five slides — the deck's spine.

**Gap 2 — "How could wanting it be what stops me?"**
- Opens: S2 (the inverted proverb). Withheld: the causal link between desire and quitting.
- Closes: S3-S4 (willpower is finite, validation loop, outcome looks far away, withdrawal). **Full reveal**, and a genuine explanation rather than an assertion.

**Gap 3 — "Or is it?"**
- Opens: S3, last line. Withheld: whether validation can in fact be immediate.
- Closes: S6 — yes, if you re-point it at output. **Full reveal.** Note she does not close it on slide 4 where the reader expects it; letting it hang three slides is the deck's best-managed tension.

**Gap 4 — "What is the way around this?"**
- Opens: S4 ("Luckily there is a way around this."). Closes: S5-S6. **Full reveal.**

**Gap 5 — "What does measuring output actually look like?"**
- Opens: S6 ("Your output." — abstract). Closes: S7's six-row table. **Full reveal, with six worked examples.**

**Gap map:** one long spine loop (S1 to S6) with three nested short loops inside it (S2 to S4, S3 to S6, S4 to S6), then one concretising loop (S6 to S7). Nothing is deferred to a DM. Nothing is held back for a paid product. **This is the only fully self-contained argument of the four decks** — and that is precisely why the comment count is 65 rather than 900: there is nothing to type for, because she already gave everything.

### WHAT SHE HIDES vs REVEALS

**She actually teaches the how.** This is the exception in the set. Free and complete:
- the cause (over-reliance on willpower, tied to a validation loop)
- the physiology (dopamine on perceived rightness; uncertainty read as threat; withdrawal as self-preservation)
- the fix (re-source validation to output)
- six executable measurement swaps (S7)
- her own before/after with the fix (S7)

**Withheld:** almost nothing of substance. What is missing is only elaboration — no protocol, no daily minimum, no tracking system, no timeframe. And notably: **no product exists in this post at all.** The caption is the only place a hint of restraint appears, and even there she gives the fix away for free: "this one thing is something that you can start doing today without paying anyone a single cent 👛."

**Verdict: taught the why AND the how.** Contrast with post 9 (why only) and posts 11/12 (why plus examples, how deferred to the vault). This deck's business function is not lead capture but **credibility and followers** — hence the about-me slide instead of a comment word.

### HOW SHE TEACHES

Four modes, in this order:
1. **Cliché inversion** (S2) — sets the frame by attacking a belief.
2. **Mechanism** (S3, S4) — actual cause-and-effect explanation, including named physiology ("dopamine", "self-preservation"). This is the only deck of the four where she genuinely explains *why* a thing happens rather than asserting that it does.
3. **Diagram** (S5) — the goal/steps drawing. It carries the argument that the route is unknowable, which is very hard to make land in prose.
4. **Contrast list / worked examples** (S7) — six instead-of pairs.

Where the actual teaching happens: **S3 and S4 hold the diagnosis, S6 holds the prescription, S7 holds the application.** S2 and S5 are framing devices, not teaching.

### SPECIFICITY & PROOF

Thin on receipts, rich on mechanism. What is concrete:
- "dopamine", "validation loop", "self-preservation" — borrowed scientific vocabulary functioning as proof-by-register. No study, no source, no citation anywhere.
- "one step. Maybe two or three if you're feeling motivated." (S4) — numeric texture in the failure story
- the six paired examples on S7, each naming a domain (followers, weight, money, days, reps, quitting)
- S8: "the country in South Australia", "a mum of 2", "20 hours to work every week" — three hard, checkable, unglamorous facts, all placed at the very end
- caption: "without paying anyone a single cent"

Personal receipt: **exactly one, and it is a process receipt rather than a results receipt** — "I found a much healthier relationship with following through on things when I refocused my attention…" (S7). No revenue, no followers, no before/after numbers. Compare with post 9's "7-figure business": this deck proves nothing about her success and everything about her having had the same problem. Different proof strategy for a different job.

### VOICE

- **Sentence length: much longer than post 9.** S4 and S5 run 25-40 word sentences with em-dash asides. Median maybe 15-18 words. The deck reads like an essay, not a list.
- **Rhythm: build-then-break.** Long explanatory sentence, long explanatory sentence, then a two-to-four word hit: "So you withdraw." / "It's biology." / "Or is it?" / "Your output." Every one of her best lines in this deck is under five words and follows a long one.
- **Second person: heavy but not total** — S4 and S5 are almost entirely "you". She also uses **"we"** for the physiology ("we stop pursuing the unknown", "way more common than we think"), which puts her inside the flaw rather than outside diagnosing it.
- **First person appears in the right place only:** S7 and S8. She earns the argument first, claims it second.
- **Questions: three, all load-bearing** — "But what if wanting it so much is the very thing stopping you…?" (S2), "Or is it?" (S3), and the implied question of the S7 table. Every question in this deck opens a loop rather than decorating a sentence.
- **Self-deprecation: explicit and charming.** "I move my hands a lot and tend to use awkward grammar." (S8) — she pre-empts criticism of her own prose, and it is the only highlighted line on that slide, i.e. she chose to emphasise her flaw. "after trying to chase invisible checklists of how I was supposed to run my business" (S8) is an admission of having been wrong.
- **Admissions of being wrong:** yes — S7 ("the ideal scenario I allowed social media to fabricate in my mind") and S8 (invisible checklists).
- **Contractions: constant.** Also colloquialism: "scale down the heck out of it" (S8).
- **Typos preserved:** "instead ofhow long it's taking..." (S7, missing space). Consistent with the awkward-grammar self-description — arguably an asset.

### CLOSING SLIDE

Slide 8 is not a CTA slide in the sales sense — it is an **introduction placed at the end**, which is a deliberate inversion. Having spent seven slides being useful, she uses the eighth to say who she is, and every fact chosen is a *de-escalation of status*:

- "I live in the country in South Australia" — not a city, not an office
- "I am a mum of 2" — competing demands
- "have 20 hours to work every week" — she names her constraint as a number, which is the single most disarming line on the slide
- "on a mission to scale down the heck out of it" — anti-growth positioning, the opposite of every business account
- "I move my hands a lot and tend to use awkward grammar" — highlighted; a flaw as a selling point

**CTA mechanism:** follow, plus a technical instruction — "tap the follow button and select 'favourite' so Instagram actually shows you my stuff. It loves to hide it otherwise." Two things worth noting. First, she teaches a platform mechanic the reader may not know, which is one last piece of free value at the moment of the ask. Second, "It loves to hide it otherwise" makes the algorithm a shared antagonist — the follow becomes a small act of complicity rather than a favour.

**No comment word. No offer. No link. No urgency.** The conversion goal of this post is audience, not leads — which explains the 65 comments cleanly. She traded comment volume for a complete, satisfying, screenshot-worthy teach. Worth noting for the swipe file that she is willing to run posts with *no* funnel attached.

### WHY IT WENT VIRAL — honest read

**Biggest single driver: slide 2 attacks a proverb the entire audience believes, and it does so in eleven words.**

"If there is a will there is a way" is furniture — nobody examines it. "But what if wanting it so much is the very thing stopping you from being consistent?" turns the reader's own effort into the suspect. That is a genuinely new thought delivered on the second slide, and it makes the rest of the deck unskippable because the reader now has an open contradiction in their head.

The supporting drivers, in order of weight:
1. **Universality of the topic.** Not niched to business, women, age, or content. Anyone who has abandoned a gym membership is the audience. Compare post 9, whose reach is capped by an age gate.
2. **It actually delivers.** Five slides of mechanism, a payoff, and six worked examples. Saves and shares on this post are earned by usefulness rather than by identification, which is a slower but stickier virality.
3. **Blame removal with a specific culprit.** "It's biology." Three words that let the reader off the hook without letting them off the task.
4. **The design break.** The sky cover in her otherwise text-on-paper feed is a pattern interrupt in the grid itself.

The honest read on the low comment count: **this post was not built to convert, and it didn't.** 7,447 likes with 65 comments is a post that was loved and not acted on. Whether that is a mistake depends on whether the follower gain was worth the forgone leads — but as a piece of craft, it is the best-argued deck of the four, and the fact that it has the fewest comments is the clearest evidence in the whole set that **comment volume is manufactured by the gap you leave, not by the quality of what you teach.**
