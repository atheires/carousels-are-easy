## PART 2 — ANALYSIS

### SLIDE 1 — THE HOOK

`Failing / Is Sexy. / Own It.` Five words, two full stops, one imperative. The most compressed hook in the four posts and the only one that is not about content or business at all.

**The promise:** not information. It promises *permission and status*. The reader is not being offered a method, they are being offered a new way to feel about something they are currently ashamed of.

**The mechanism of the hook is lexical collision.** "Failing" and "sexy" do not belong in the same sentence, and the friction between them is the entire device. Failure carries shame; sexy carries desirability and status. Jamming them together produces a claim the reader cannot immediately evaluate, so they have to swipe to find out whether she means it. Note that "sexy" is doing something more precise than shock value — it reframes failure as *attractive to others*, not merely *acceptable to yourself*. That is a much larger promise, and it is why the word beats every safer synonym (brave, valuable, necessary).

`Own It.` then converts an observation into an instruction, so the slide contains both a claim and an action in five words.

**What curiosity it opens:** in what sense could failing possibly be sexy — and who says so?

**What it hides:** the argument, the evidence, and the register. From slide 1 you cannot tell whether this is a personal story, a psychology explainer, or a case-study post. It turns out to be the third, which is the least guessable option.

**The design amplifies it.** The yellow highlight lands on `Is Sexy.` — at thumbnail size the eye reads *Failing / IS SEXY* with the highlight functioning as emphasis on the impossible half of the sentence. And the choice of full stops rather than a colon or ellipsis matters: p15 used `Read This:` to promise continuation, but this hook does the opposite — it closes itself, and the swipe is driven by disagreement rather than by an unfinished sentence.

### THE MESSAGE

She is arguing: **failure is not a verdict on you, it is evidence about your ambition — and the women you admire have more of it than you do, not less.**

- **Reader at slide 1:** "My failures are proof that I am not good enough at this. The successful people I compare myself to did not have these problems. My bad numbers mean something about me."
- **Reader at slide 9:** "Failure is the cost of attempting things, and the women I admire paid it repeatedly and publicly. My setbacks are information about what does not work, not information about my worth. My numbers are feedback, not identity. And the fact that I keep failing means I am attempting more than the people who are not failing."

Two arguments run in parallel and the second is the more commercially relevant one. Argument one, slides 2 to 6: **failure is universal among the successful** — evidenced entirely by borrowed authority. Argument two, slides 7 and 8: **failure must be processed as data rather than absorbed as identity** — and slide 8 quietly converts the whole abstract discussion into the reader's actual daily wound, which is not the LSAT or a lawsuit but low engagement on a post.

Slide 8 is the hinge of the post's usefulness. Everything before it is inspiration about billionaires; slide 8 is about the reader's own analytics tab. `Low engagement =/= Bad content` is the sentence that makes this post land on a creator rather than on a general audience.

### SLIDE-BY-SLIDE PROGRESSION

**Slide 1 — Provocation hook.** Hands off through disbelief. No open question, no colon — the reader swipes to test a claim.

**Slide 2 — Establish the enemy belief.** Job: name the stigma precisely before attacking it, so the reader recognises their own conditioning. Transition device: **anaphora then a question-pivot.** Three parallel fragments — `Something to hide.` / `Something to be embarrassed about.` / `Something that means we're not good enough.` — each one escalating from behaviour to identity. Note `We've been taught`, which locates the belief outside the reader: this is conditioning, not character. Then the turn: `But the most successful women? / They collect failures like trophies.` The rhetorical question mid-slide is the swivel point, and `collect failures like trophies` is the image the rest of the carousel literally executes — slides 4, 5 and 6 *are* the trophy cabinet. Handoff: an unproven claim about successful women, which obliges her to name some.

**Slide 3 — The reframe, stated as a mechanism.** Transition device: **explicit instruction pair.** `Stop seeing failure as judgment. / Start seeing failure as proof.` Stop/start is the cleanest possible reframe grammar. Then the word "proof" is repeated three times in anaphora — `Proof that you tried. / Proof that you're pushing boundaries. / Proof that you're not playing it safe.` — which converts an abstract instruction into three specific self-descriptions the reader can adopt. Then the triple restatement in the vocabulary of research: `Every failure is data. Every setback is information. Every 'no' is research.` Three synonyms in one line is over-writing, but it is over-writing with a purpose: it installs a vocabulary. Handoff: the reframe is asserted but unevidenced, so evidence must follow.

**Slide 4 — Proof 1: Sara Blakely.** Job: prove the trophy claim with the least arguable possible example. Transition device: **the failure list before the outcome.** She leads with `failed the LSAT twice`, `selling fax machines door-to-door`, `repeated refusals from hosiery manufacturers` — three humiliations, specific and unglamorous — and only then reveals `billion-dollar success`. Sequencing failure first and success last is what makes the slide an argument rather than a compliment. The direct quote closes it: `"Failure is not the outcome - failure is not trying."` — which is the carousel's thesis in Blakely's mouth rather than Tinna's, and therefore unarguable. Handoff: one example is an anecdote, so a second is required.

**Slide 5 — Proof 2: Oprah.** Job: escalate from commercial failure to *personal rejection about who you are*. The Blakely failures were about a product; the Oprah failures are about her temperament — `fired... for being 'too emotionally invested'`, `'unfit for television news'`. That is a much closer match to the reader's actual fear, which is not that the business fails but that she is personally unsuited. Transition device: **the one-word antithesis.** `She didn't let rejection define her. / She let it refine her.` Define/refine — a single letter apart, and the most quotable line on the slide. Then the long verbatim quote, and note what she chose: Oprah *agreeing* with the criticism. `I was not a good television reporter. I was too emotional.` Using a quote where the icon concedes the fault is far stronger than one where she dismisses it, because it models the behaviour the carousel is asking for — hold the failure without shame.

**Slide 6 — Proof 3: Whitney Wolfe Herd. The longest slide in the carousel.** Job: escalate a third time, to public humiliation and betrayal, and add revenge as an emotion. `got caught in a mess of conflict and lawsuits, and had to walk away from the company she helped build` — the only example involving being pushed out rather than turned down. Transition device: **the fuel metaphor.** `she used it as fuel. That "failure" became the fire behind Bumble` — and the scare-quotes on `"failure"` retroactively argue it was never a failure at all. Then the escalation beyond recovery: `She didn't just bounce back. She rewrote the rules of an industry that was never built for women in the first place.` That last clause converts a personal story into a structural grievance, the most shareable sentence on the slide for this audience. The quote closes with agency: `I took my power back.`

**Slide 7 — The method. The only actionable slide.** Transition device: **hard tonal shift to bullet imperatives.** Five verbs — Track, Remove, Look for, Use, Celebrate. `Track what didn't work and why` / `Remove emotional judgment from the data` / `Look for patterns in your 'failures'` / `Use each setback to refine your approach` / `Celebrate the courage it took to try`. The sequence is a genuine loop: record, de-emotionalise, analyse, adjust, reward. Note that `Celebrate the courage it took to try` is last, so the emotional payoff is positioned as the final step of a process rather than as a substitute for one. Ends on the weakest line in the carousel: `Make failure your building buddy.` The alliteration is childish and undercuts the slide's authority — compare slide 2's `collect failures like trophies`, which does the same job far better.

**Slide 8 — The landing slide. Where the post becomes about the reader's real life.** Transition device: **the equation table.** Four lines using the mathematical not-equal sign — `Low engagement =/= Bad content` / `Few sales =/= Wrong business` / `Small audience =/= No impact` / `Slow growth =/= Failure`. The most visually distinct slide in the carousel and the only one using symbolic notation, which makes it the most screenshot-friendly. Each equation severs a specific inference the reader makes daily. Then the two-line law: `Your numbers are feedback, not judgment. / Use them as information, not identity.` The judgment/feedback pair is a direct callback to slide 3's `judgment` versus `proof`, so the opening reframe is restated in the reader's own vocabulary of analytics. **If I had to name the single most useful slide across all four posts for this audience, it is this one.**

**Slide 9 — Payoff / the title answered.** Transition device: **the direct callback headline.** `Why Failing Is Actually Sexy` — "Actually" acknowledges that the reader arrived sceptical. Then five reasons, all second person, escalating from character to competitive advantage: `brave enough to try`, `not settling for mediocre`, `pushing boundaries`, `learning faster than your competition`, `building resilience that can't be taught`. The fourth introduces *other people* for the first time — competition — and the closer completes that turn: `While others play it safe, you're playing to win.` The final beat is comparative, not internal. The carousel began by removing shame and ends by conferring superiority, which is exactly what "sexy" promised on slide 1 and which "brave" or "valuable" could not have delivered.

**Slide 10 — missing.** Given the caption's closing question and the pattern of the other three posts, it is most likely that question rendered as a slide, or a soft CTA. Its absence means the offer mechanics cannot be assessed here — and with only 49 comments, whatever was on slide 10 evidently did not carry a comment word.

### KNOWLEDGE GAPS

**Five gaps across the nine available slides. Structure: the shallowest gap architecture of the four posts — she opens few loops and closes almost all of them. This is a belief post, not a curiosity post.**

**Gap 1 — in what sense is failing sexy (spine)**
- Opens: Slide 1, the provocation. Withheld: the entire argument and its register.
- Closes: Slide 9, explicitly, with a five-item answer under the headline `Why Failing Is Actually Sexy`.
- Completeness: **full, and formally closed by title callback.** The carousel is bookended: slide 1 asserts, slide 9 justifies. The only one of the four posts whose final available slide answers its own hook.

**Gap 2 — who are these successful women who collect failures**
- Opens: Slide 2, `But the most successful women? / They collect failures like trophies.` Withheld: names.
- Closes: Slides 4, 5, 6 — one woman per slide.
- Completeness: **full, and generously.** Three named examples with specifics and verbatim quotes. Structurally identical to p13's four empty circles: a plural claim on one slide, cashed one item per slide afterwards. Three guaranteed swipes bought with one rhetorical question.

**Gap 3 — how do I actually treat failure as data**
- Opens: Slide 3, `Every failure is data` — asserted with no procedure.
- Closes: Slide 7, with a five-step loop.
- Completeness: **full, and this is the post's real gift.** She waits four slides to close it, the longest hold in the carousel, and fills the interval with the three case studies.

**Gap 4 — but my numbers say I am failing**
- Opens: never explicitly — it is the reader's silent objection throughout.
- Closes: Slide 8, with four equations and a two-line law.
- Completeness: **full.** The most impressive gap-handling in the post: she closes an objection the reader never stated, which produces the feeling of being read rather than taught.

**Gap 5 — what is my own failure worth**
- Opens: Slide 9's five flattering reasons.
- Closes: **routed to the caption question** (`what's one failure that ended up being the biggest stepping stone for you so far`).
- Completeness: **deliberately handed back to the reader.** She does not answer it because the reader answering it is the engagement mechanism.

**Shape of the gap map:** one spine gap held from slide 1 to slide 9, two fully-paid sub-gaps inside it (the three names, the five-step method), and one silent objection ambushed on slide 8. **Nothing is withheld for an offer.** No product, no method name, no masterclass, no comment word in the slides. This is the only one of the four posts not selling anything, and the gap structure reflects that completely — every loop she opens, she closes.

### WHAT SHE HIDES vs REVEALS

**Revealed free:** the entire argument; three named case studies with concrete, checkable failures (LSAT twice, fax machines door-to-door, hosiery manufacturer refusals, fired from a TV job, ousted from Tinder amid lawsuits); three verbatim quotes; a complete five-step processing method; four specific metric reframes; five reasons failure confers advantage.

**Withheld:** almost nothing of substance. No offer, no method name, no paid product, no next step. The only thing she withholds is **her own failure story** — striking, given that p15 is built entirely on hers. This post about failure contains not one of her failures. She borrows Blakely, Oprah and Wolfe Herd instead.

**So: WHY or HOW?** This is the most **complete** of the four posts. She teaches the why (slides 2-6), the how (slide 7), and the application (slide 8), and closes with the payoff (slide 9). Nothing is routed to a DM. It is a pure value post — which is precisely why it has 49 comments against p14's 518 and p15's 490. **The lesson is unambiguous and worth writing down: the post with the most complete teaching and the fewest open loops got roughly one tenth the comments of the two posts that withheld their central mechanism.** Likes held up (5,436, in line with the others), so it was read and appreciated. It just gave the reader nothing to do.

### HOW SHE TEACHES

- **Borrowed authority / case study** — the dominant device, three of nine slides. Each follows an identical internal shape: failures listed first, outcome second, verbatim quote third. That repetition is what makes slides 4-6 read as a body of evidence rather than three anecdotes.
- **Direct quotation** — three verbatim quotes with named attribution, set in italics. The only one of the four posts to quote anyone but herself and a customer.
- **Reframe by stop/start instruction** — slide 3.
- **Anaphora** — slide 2 (`Something to...` x3), slide 3 (`Proof that...` x3), slide 8 (`=/=` x4), slide 9 (`You're...` x3). Heavy repetition-based construction throughout; it makes the copy chantable.
- **Equation / notation** — slide 8, unique in the set.
- **Checklist of imperatives** — slide 7.
- **Antithesis** — `define her / refine her` (s5), `feedback, not judgment` (s8), `information, not identity` (s8), `didn't just bounce back / rewrote the rules` (s6).
- **No story of her own, no diagram, no metaphor beyond trophies, fire and the weak building buddy.**

**Actual teaching happens in slides 3, 7 and 8.** Slides 4, 5 and 6 are evidence. Slide 9 is reward.

### VOICE

- **Sentence length:** short and highly patterned. Median around 7 words in the reframe slides, longer in the case-study slides (the Wolfe Herd paragraph runs past 30 words) because biography needs clauses.
- **Rhythm:** the most repetitive of the four posts, deliberately. `Something to hide. / Something to be embarrassed about. / Something that means we're not good enough.` then `Proof that you tried. / Proof that you're pushing boundaries. / Proof that you're not playing it safe.` then four equations then five `You're...` lines. It reads like something meant to be said aloud. A real stylistic departure from p14's conversational fragments.
- **Second person:** dominant in slides 3, 7, 8, 9. Absent from slides 4, 5, 6, which are third person (`She failed...`, `She didn't let...`). The alternation is the post's architecture: *you* slides make the claim, *she* slides prove it.
- **First person:** **entirely absent from the slides.** No `I`, no `my`, nowhere. Unique among the four posts.
- **Contractions:** yes but fewer — `We've`, `didn't`, `you're`, `can't`. The register is a touch more formal than p14 and p15, which fits the case-study material.
- **Questions:** two, both rhetorical pivots: `But the most successful women?` (s2) and the implied question in `Why Failing Is Actually Sexy` (s9).
- **Profanity:** none. Compare p15's two deployments. The provocation here is carried by the word "sexy" instead, doing the same job — a register violation signalling she is not going to be careful with you.
- **Self-deprecation and admissions of being wrong:** none on the slides. Her authority here is curatorial rather than experiential — she is the person who assembled the evidence, not the person who lived it.
- **Typos and roughness left in:** `skills she that helped her on her path` (slide 4, garbled), missing punctuation at the end of slide 5's second bullet, single-versus-double quote inconsistency. Same lack of polish as the other three.
- **Weakest voice moment:** `Make failure your building buddy.` The only line in the four posts that sounds written by a brand rather than a person.

### CLOSING SLIDE

**Caveat: slide 10 was not captured.** Assessing slide 9 as the last available frame:

Slide 9 is a **payoff slide, not a CTA slide.** Its job is to answer the hook. `Why Failing Is Actually Sexy` restates slide 1's provocation as a question and answers it in five lines, escalating from `brave enough to try` to `playing to win`. The final sentence — `While others play it safe, you're playing to win.` — converts the whole carousel from consolation into status. That is the correct emotional destination for a post whose hook promised sexiness rather than comfort.

**CTA mechanism: none in the visible slides.** No comment word, no product, no link, no method name. The entire ask lives in the caption: `let me know below what's one failure that ended up being the biggest stepping stone for you so far`.

**How she converts attention: she mostly does not.** The numbers say so plainly. 5,436 likes against **49 comments** — about 111:1, versus roughly 12:1 for both p14 and p15. Read and liked at the same rate as the others, and barely a tenth of the conversation. Two probable causes, both craft lessons rather than accidents:

1. **No comment word.** p13, p14 and p15 all give a single word to type in exchange for something. This one asks for a considered personal disclosure instead — a far higher-effort request with no reward attached.
2. **She goes first nowhere.** The caption asks the reader to name a failure, but neither the slides nor the caption contain one of hers. Compare p15, which opens with `I threw away a successful photography business` and earns 490 comments. Reciprocity is the mechanism, and this post skips it.

The uncomfortable conclusion, stated plainly for the swipe file: **this is the best-taught and most generous of the four posts, and the worst-performing on engagement.** Completeness satisfies the reader and gives them nothing to do.

**Roughly 110 words, the longest caption of the four. It does something none of the others do: it introduces an entirely new story that appears nowhere in the slides.**

- **Opening line:** `#sarablakely dad would ask her and her brother at the dinner table "what did you fail at today?"` A hashtag used as the first word, functioning as the sentence's subject — grammatically odd, but it means the caption opens with a recognisable name and a concrete domestic scene. The dinner-table detail is the most vivid image anywhere in the post, slides included.
- **Relationship to carousel:** it **extends with new material.** Slide 4 covers Blakely's failures; the caption covers her *upbringing* — a different, earlier, more transferable story. The slides argue that failure is retrospectively useful; the caption shows a household where failure was prospectively *required*. `He'd be disappointed if they had nothing to report.` That inversion is stronger than anything on slides 2 to 6, and it is buried in a caption.
- **Her own reframe of the anecdote:** `It's almost like saying - ok what are you getting better at next time you try?` The `ok` is a spoken filler marking the shift from reporting to interpreting — her voice re-entering, and the only place in the whole post where it does.
- **The best sentence in the post:** `Your fear can't live in the possibility of failing. It needs to stay in the scenario where you just don't even try.` This is a genuine relocation of fear rather than a denial of it — she does not tell the reader to be unafraid, she tells them to point the fear at inaction instead. A more sophisticated psychological move than any slide makes, and it belonged on a slide.
- **CTA:** `So let me know below what's one failure that ended up being the biggest stepping stone for you so far` plus a thinking-face emoji. High-effort, high-vulnerability, low-reward, and with no anonymity reassurance (contrast p14's `I can't see who answers what`). 49 comments is the predictable result.
- **Hashtags:** one, `#sarablakely`, load-bearing as a word rather than used for reach. No hashtag block. Consistent across all four posts — she does not use hashtags for distribution.
- **Fuller story?** Yes, and this is the sharpest craft observation available here: **the caption contains the best material in the post.** The dinner-table ritual and the fear-relocation sentence are both stronger than the slide copy they support, and both sit where fewest people read.

### WHY IT WENT VIRAL

**The single biggest driver: slide 8, reached via three borrowed billionaires.**

The hook gets the swipe through lexical collision (`Failing Is Sexy.`), and the three case studies build permission through undeniable, specific, checkable humiliations — `failed the LSAT twice`, `unfit for television news`, `had to walk away from the company she helped build`. But the reason the post was liked 5,436 times is slide 8. `Low engagement =/= Bad content / Few sales =/= Wrong business / Small audience =/= No impact / Slow growth =/= Failure.` Those four lines address the private, daily, slightly embarrassing wound of every creator in her audience — opening the analytics tab and reading a number as a verdict on themselves. Slides 4-6 are about billionaires; slide 8 is about *you, this morning*. The borrowed authority exists to make slide 8 believable.

**Secondary driver: the audience-fit of the examples.** Three women, and each failure is a different flavour of the reader's specific fear — Blakely's is *my idea is rejected*, Oprah's is *I am the wrong kind of person*, Wolfe Herd's is *I get pushed out of what I built*. That is not three examples of one thing, it is one example of three things, so almost every reader finds herself in one of them.

**And the honest read on its underperformance:** it is the best-constructed argument of the four posts and it converted the least, because it withheld nothing and asked for something expensive. No comment word, no offer, and no vulnerability of her own to trigger reciprocity. Side by side with p15 — same audience, same design system, same length, comparable likes, 490 comments versus 49 — the difference is that p15 gave the reader one word to type and went first with its own confession. That comparison is the most valuable thing in this file.
