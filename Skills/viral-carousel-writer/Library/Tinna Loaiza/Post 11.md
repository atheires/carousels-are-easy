## PART 2 — ANALYSIS

### SLIDE 1 — THE HOOK

"Storytelling I Hardly See Anyone Using"

**Mechanism: scarcity of knowledge, asserted from a position of having watched everyone.**

Five words do the work: "I Hardly See Anyone Using". Not a promise of results, an outcome, a number or a benefit. It is a claim about **the market's ignorance**, and it implies three things at once:
- there exist storytelling approaches you do not know about (a gap)
- almost nobody is using them, so they are un-saturated — an edge
- she sits above the field watching what everyone does (authority by observation)

Word choice matters. "Hardly" rather than "never" is a hedge that makes the claim survivable — she is not saying it is a secret, she is saying it is rare. Same de-risking instinct as post 10's "(almost)".

**What it hides:** the plural. The headline says "Storytelling" — singular, uncountable — so the reader cannot tell whether one technique or twelve are coming. It also hides the *type* of thing (technique, mindset, format, framework), which means there is no way to guess the contents and therefore no way to decide it is already known.

**The curiosity opened is competitive, not aspirational.** Post 9's hook offers relief; this one offers **advantage**. The implicit reader thought is not "help me" but "what does she know that my competitors don't?"

Also note: the handle appears on slide 1, unlike posts 9 and 12. She expects this one to be screenshotted and shared out of context.

### THE MESSAGE

**What she is arguing:** your stories are not too small to tell — the small, unresolved, unflattering, off-topic ones are the ones that build trust, and there are named categories of them you are currently ignoring.

**Reader at the start:** "I don't have good stories. My life isn't interesting enough. I'll post a story when something big happens — a launch, a win, a transformation. Right now I have nothing worth telling."

**Reader at the end:** "I have five kinds of stories sitting unused: a moment before a decision, an internal conflict, something a random person taught me, a thing I'm in the middle of, and a time I got it wrong. Not having a big win is not a content problem. And there are twenty more of these behind one comment."

The deeper argument, never stated as a thesis: **trust is built in the before and the during, not the after.** She says it most directly on S3 — "most people only tell the 'after'… but that internal conflict that took place before the win, is where the trust is build." Slides 2, 4, 5 and 6 are all that same principle applied to different raw material.

### SLIDE-BY-SLIDE PROGRESSION

**S1 — Rarity claim.** No topic, no list count, no promise. Hand-off: pure curiosity plus a competitive itch. There is no grammatical cliffhanger like post 9's colon; the swipe is earned entirely by the value of the withheld information.

**S2 — Technique 1: Micro Moments.** Also carries the deck's thesis: "Not every story has to be dramatically life-changing… connection is built on something far deeper than that." Hand-off device: **the example scrap.** "I was standing in line for coffee when I heard a woman say, 'I wish I could just start already.' That one sentence sparked an entire product." It is so concrete and so obviously reusable that it establishes the deck's pattern — instruction then proof — and the reader swipes to collect the next one. **The format itself becomes the reason to continue.**

**S3 — Technique 2: Invisible Battles.** Escalates from external smallness (a coffee queue) to internal exposure (fear, doubt). Contains the deck's most quotable structural insight: most people only tell the "after". Hand-off: she has just modelled vulnerability in her own example ("I was scared of what my friends might think"), which raises the stakes for the next slide.

**S4 — Technique 3: Unlikely Teacher.** The centrepiece, and the only technique with a dated real receipt: "I for example wrote an email a few weeks back about a pasta with prawns my husband cooked for dinner." The scrap runs six lines and lands a real metaphor: "I've been living my whole life like that damn bowl of pasta." Hand-off: this slide proves she does the thing she teaches, which retroactively validates S2-S3 and pre-validates S5-S6.

**S5 — Technique 4: Unfinished Stories.** Shortest slide in the deck. "Having your sh*t together is very overrated." Hand-off: the scrap ("I'm mid-pivot. It's messy.") is an admission about her *current* business, which sets up the final technique's demand for a full confession.

**S6 — Technique 5: Flawed Hero.** The most exposing of the five. The scrap is the best example in the deck because it contains a real failure with a real cost — "I told a client to double her prices. She did and got no sales." — *and* an open loop: "Here's what I missed and what we changed." She demonstrates, on a scrap of paper, that a story can end on a hook. Hand-off: escalation is complete, so the deck must resolve into an offer.

**S7 — CTA.** Reframes the whole deck as a learnable skill tied to money ("the people ready to buy the thing you sell"), then makes the trade: comment "storyline" for a vault of 20 prompts.

**Structural read: a stack with an escalation gradient.** The five techniques are logically independent — you could reorder them — but they are not randomly ordered. They run from least exposing to most exposing: an overheard sentence, then a private fear, then a domestic anecdote, then an unfinished pivot, then a professional failure with a named client outcome. Each slide asks the reader for slightly more courage than the last. That covert gradient is what makes a parallel list feel like it builds.

### KNOWLEDGE GAPS

**Total: 8 — the highest of the four decks. Structure: five identical micro-gaps in parallel, one spine gap, two open-ended.**

**Gap 1 (spine) — "What is the storytelling nobody uses?"**
- Opens: S1. Withheld: the entire subject.
- Closes: **incrementally across S2-S6**, one technique per slide; never at a single point.
- Completeness: **partial by design.** Five techniques are revealed, then S7 reveals there are twenty. The spine gap is *widened* at the moment it appears satisfied. This is the sharpest structural move in the deck: she does not withhold the answer, she **reveals a quarter of it and prices the rest at one comment.**

**Gaps 2-6 (parallel micro-gaps), one per technique.** Each slide opens the same shape of gap with its title and closes it in the same slide with body + scrap:
- "What is a Micro Moment?" (S2) — closed fully, in-slide, with example
- "What is an Invisible Battle?" (S3) — closed fully, in-slide, with example
- "What is an Unlikely Teacher?" (S4) — closed fully, plus a dated receipt
- "What is an Unfinished Story?" (S5) — closed fully
- "What is a Flawed Hero?" (S6) — closed fully

All five are **title-as-gap, body-as-close** with a one-slide lifespan. This is why the deck reads fast and never feels stingy: the reader is paid on every slide. Open-close, open-close, five times.

**Gap 7 (embedded, deliberately unresolved) — "What did she miss with the client who doubled her prices?"**
- Opens: S6's scrap ("Here's what I missed and what we changed.")
- Withheld: the actual lesson. She writes the *hook* of a story and never tells the story.
- Closes: **never.** Completeness: zero.
- This is genuinely clever: the unresolved example is itself a demonstration of the technique. The reader feels the pull of an unfinished story, which proves her claim better than any explanation could.

**Gap 8 — "What are the other fifteen prompts?"**
- Opens: S7 ("a vault with 20 prompts and examples just like these ones"). Closes: never on-platform. **Fully deferred to the comment word.**
- Note the precision of "just like these ones": she is not promising something *better* than what she gave, she is promising *more of the same* — a far easier promise to believe after five demonstrated examples.

**Gap map:** five self-closing micro-loops in a row (maximum satisfaction per swipe), sitting inside one spine loop that is deliberately re-opened wider at the CTA (maximum motivation to comment), plus one never-closed embedded loop on S6 that doubles as proof of method. **The deck gives generously and asks precisely** — five full payoffs, then a quantified remainder. That is why it carries 988 comments.

### WHAT SHE HIDES vs REVEALS

**Revealed, and this is unusually generous:** five complete, named, usable techniques, each with a written example she does not paraphrase but *writes out in full*. A reader can close this carousel and produce a post today from any of the five. That is real, transferable craft given away free.

**Withheld:**
- the other fifteen prompts (quantified, priced at one comment)
- any craft *beyond* selection — she teaches you *what* to look for but never *how to write it*. There is nothing here about structure, sentence rhythm, where to put the turn, how to open, how to land. The deck is a **sourcing** guide masquerading as a storytelling guide.
- the resolution of the S6 client story
- any results data: no engagement numbers, no sales figures, no proof that these five categories outperform anything

**Verdict: she teaches the WHAT and hides the HOW.** This is a third position, distinct from post 9 (why only) and post 10 (why plus how). Her five techniques answer "which of my experiences count as stories" — genuinely the hardest and most blocking question for the reader — while leaving execution untouched. Cleverly, this makes the free content feel complete while leaving the product with something real to sell.

### HOW SHE TEACHES

**Almost entirely by example.** The teaching architecture is identical on all five slides:
1. a **named label** (Micro Moments, Invisible Battles, Unlikely Teacher, Unfinished Stories, Flawed Hero) — naming is doing heavy lifting; a nameless category is unmemorable, a named one is retrievable
2. **an instruction** in the imperative ("Share the story of the 10 seconds before you made a decision", "Tell a story where a kid, random person, movie, or mistake taught you any type of lesson")
3. **a one-line reason** ("Unexpected sources create tension and curiosity", "This will humanise your brand and build trust based on your willingness to admit a mistake")
4. **a written example on a torn scrap** — not a description of an example, the example itself

The actual teaching lives in **the five scraps**, not the body copy. Strip the scraps and the deck collapses into vague advice. The scraps are what make it screenshot-worthy and what make the "20 prompts and examples" promise credible.

Modes used: naming, imperative instruction, one-line mechanism, worked example. Modes not used: story (she never tells a full narrative anywhere in the deck), diagram, data, contrast list, personal transformation arc.

Numerals used in the whole deck: "10 seconds" (S2), "a few weeks back" (S4), "20 prompts" (S7). **Three**, versus twelve-plus in post 9 — and yet this deck feels far better evidenced, because the proof is texture rather than arithmetic.

Personal receipts: **five, one per technique slide**, and four are unflattering (a fear, a mess, a mid-pivot, a client failure). She pays for the right to teach vulnerability by being vulnerable inside the teaching. Absent: engagement data, revenue, testimonials, follower counts, client names.

### VOICE

- **Sentence length: medium and structurally loose.** Body copy runs 12-25 words with clauses stacked by commas rather than periods: "You know those stories about you changing your mind, doubting yourself, or that tiny feeling of resistance to something." Spoken syntax written down.
- **The scraps use a different voice from the body.** Body copy is instructional second person ("Tell a story where…", "Try telling a story that's…"). Scraps are first-person past tense, fragmentary and rhythmically much better: "I was rationing the prawns. Saving them." / "I'm mid-pivot. It's messy." Two-word sentences appear only inside the scraps. She writes better when she is being a person than when she is being a teacher, and the design admits it by putting the scraps on separate paper.
- **Grammar visibly imperfect and left uncorrected:** "is where the trust is build" (S3), the dropped "but" in the S3 scrap, the two-dot ".." after "after", "I for example wrote an email" (S4 word order). In a deck arguing for unpolished honesty this is congruent — and it is also simply how she writes (see post 10's "I tend to use awkward grammar").
- **Second person dominant in instruction, absent in examples.** Perfect division of labour: "you" for what to do, "I" for proof it works.
- **Self-deprecation and admissions of being wrong: highest of the four decks.** "I was scared of what my friends might think" (S3), "It's messy" (S5), "Here's what I missed" (S6). S6 in particular admits giving a client advice that lost them sales — a real professional risk, taken on purpose, because the argument requires it.
- **Contractions: constant.** Profanity twice: "shit" uncensored (S3, inside a scrap) and "sh*t" censored (S5, in her own body copy). The inconsistency is revealing — she censors in her own voice, not in a quoted example.
- **Questions: zero.** Not one question mark in seven slides. Unlike post 10, which runs on questions, this deck runs on imperatives: "Share…", "Tell…", "Try telling…", "Comment…". A briefing, not a conversation.

### CLOSING SLIDE

Slide 7 does three things, and the design change signals all of them: text centres for the first time, body copy shrinks, and the book page becomes visible behind — the frame goes quiet.

1. **It renames the deck's subject as a capability tied to revenue.** "You can learn to tell stories that stand out and actually connect with the people ready to buy the thing you sell." Two claims folded together: this is learnable (so you are not untalented) and it is commercial (so this is not a hobby). "the people ready to buy" quietly does targeting work — not an audience, buyers.
2. **It makes the trade explicit and cheap.** "Comment 'storyline' and I'll share a vault with 20 prompts and examples just like these ones." "Vault" implies a stored, finite, valuable collection rather than a PDF. "Just like these ones" anchors the promised value to the five she already proved.
3. **It sets the ratio.** Five given, twenty offered. The reader has just experienced the sample quality, so 20 is not abstract — it is four times something already valued.

CTA mechanism: comment-to-DM. **Note what is absent:** no offer, no price, no masterclass, no link, no urgency, no follow ask. Pure lead capture with a single friction point — type one lowercase word.

The lowercase "storyline" on the slide versus uppercase "STORYLINE" in the caption is a small inconsistency; the highlight makes the slide version findable either way.

The caption speaks to a **founder with a CMO and a content team**, and argues something the deck never mentions: storytelling cannot be delegated, and outsourcing it is why offers feel disconnected and sales fall. A strategic business argument, not a craft argument.

The most opinionated opening line of the four posts, and it works as a second hook for a higher-status reader.
- **Structure:** seven short paragraphs escalating from observation to consequence. Longest caption of the four by a wide margin, roughly 190 words.
- **Its own argument chain:** corporations get it wrong, it cannot be found in client reviews, it must come from the founder's perspective, you may delegate the writing but not the thread, most owners lose the thread early, and the symptoms are disconnected offers, falling bookings and panic strategy pivots. That final paragraph is a **cost-of-inaction paragraph** and it appears nowhere in the slides.
- **CTA: double, and share-first.** "consider sharing it" comes *before* "comment 'STORYLINE'". The slides asked only for the comment. The conditional framing ("if you liked this or found it useful") is softer than the slide's flat instruction.
- **Sign-off:** "Thank you for reading. / Tinna" — a letter close. Nobody signs an Instagram caption. It frames the whole thing as correspondence, and it is consistent with the deck's aged-paper, torn-scrap, book-page design language. Design and sign-off tell the same story: this is a letter, not a post.
- **Typos:** "This not a thing", "is build", "loosing", "most businesses owner", "loose sight", "resulting into". Six errors in 190 words, published untouched by a woman selling communication skills — and it plainly does not hurt her, because the ideas are sharp and the register is human.
- **Hashtags:** none.

**Fuller story?** Yes, materially. The caption supplies the *stakes* the deck omits (lost bookings, disconnected offers, panicked strategy changes) and a *positioning argument* (founder-owned storytelling) that reframes the five techniques from "content ideas" into "the thing your marketing cannot outsource." The deck sells ease; the caption sells consequence.

### WHY IT WENT VIRAL — honest read

**Biggest single driver: it solves the one problem that actually blocks the reader — "I have nothing to write about" — and it solves it five times over with examples that can be copied today.**

Almost all storytelling content teaches *structure* (hook, tension, resolution). Structure is useless to someone who believes she has no material. Tinna skips structure entirely and attacks **sourcing**: here are five categories of thing that already happened to you, and here is what each looks like written out. The five scraps are the engine of the post — they convert "storytelling" from an art the reader lacks into a filing problem the reader can solve.

Reinforcing drivers, in weight order:
1. **The named labels are memorable and repeatable.** "Micro Moments", "Invisible Battles", "Flawed Hero". Naming makes ideas portable — a saver can recall the deck by its five labels a week later, and a sharer has language to describe it.
2. **Payoff on every slide.** Five self-contained gaps opened and closed. No slide asks the reader to wait, so drop-off has no natural point.
3. **She takes the risk she is prescribing.** "I told a client to double her prices. She did and got no sales." A creator who teaches vulnerability while staying invulnerable does not get believed. That example is the trust anchor of the post.
4. **The five-to-twenty ratio at the CTA.** Having proven quality, the ask is trivially cheap and the remainder is quantified. 988 comments on 7,232 likes is roughly a 13.7% comment-to-like ratio — very high, and engineered by that ratio rather than by curiosity.
5. **The design carries a second signal.** Torn scraps and creased paper visually argue for imperfection before a word is read, and the instruction/example split makes a dense deck feel skimmable.

Honest caveat: the deck teaches nothing about actual writing. Its perceived value exceeds its instructional payload — but because sourcing genuinely is the reader's blocker, the value is real rather than illusory, and the vault has a legitimate job left to do.
