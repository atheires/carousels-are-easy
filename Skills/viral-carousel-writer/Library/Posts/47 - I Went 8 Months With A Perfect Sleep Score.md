## Notes

**The cover sells a result before it sells a routine.** “I went 8 months with a perfect sleep score.” makes duration part of the achievement: this is presented as repeatability, not one good night. “These are the 5 cutoffs I followed:” immediately narrows the possible explanation to five boundaries. The hook challenges the assumption that better sleep begins with something added at bedtime; “cutoffs” promises decisions about when ordinary activities stop. The score is the writer’s claim, not evidence supplied in the carousel.

**Slide 2 makes the first cutoff surprising enough to reset the reader’s timeline.** “10-12hrs before bed” reaches well into the day, before the reader would usually classify an activity as bedtime preparation. “Try to have final caffeine.” supplies the action first, and “Caffeine has a half-life of ~5-6 hours” supplies the reason second. The unusual distance from bedtime becomes less arbitrary once the reader has been given a mechanism for something remaining in the body.

**The coffee calculation translates the explanation into a picture the reader can feel.** “A cup of coffee 6hrs before bed = half a cup in your system when your head hits the pillow.” follows the technical “half-life” with an ordinary cup and an ordinary bedtime moment. The equals sign compresses the inference. “Head hits the pillow” brings the consequence to the exact instant the reader wants sleep, and “lingering” makes a daytime drink feel present rather than finished.

**The sequence moves by a clock, not a chain of rhetorical questions.** “10-12hrs,” “4-6hrs,” “4hrs,” “2hrs” and “1hr before bed” count down towards the same event. Caffeine precedes exercise because its requested boundary is earlier; food follows exercise; environmental light precedes screens. This is an **Inventory organised as a countdown**. It answers “when should I stop?” repeatedly, making five different behaviours retrievable as one evening timeline.

**The central reframe is that sleep competes with unfinished daytime demands.** “Eating close to bed wrecks your sleep & creates a large metabolic demand on your body” makes dinner another activity still being processed. After caffeine and exercise, food expands the idea beyond stimulants or exertion. “Resting heart rate spikes,” “Blood glucose fluctuates” and “Melatonin production reduces” present three consequences of the same conflict. The writing borrows the authority of physiological terms; the carousel does not show sources for these statements.

**The verbs become firmer as the clock gets closer to bed.** “Try to have” and “Try to finish” allow some latitude in the earlier cutoffs. “Finish eating.” removes the qualifier, then “Avoid blue light” and “Turn off screens” become direct environmental instructions. The brief action comes before the explanation each time, so a reader scanning the post can retrieve the cutoff without rereading its rationale. “Wreck” and “jacks” keep the explanatory voice forceful rather than clinical.

**The last two items distinguish what screens emit from what scrolling does.** “Blue light suppresses your body's ability to produce melatonin” establishes the light argument first. “Blue light delays melatonin. Doomscrolling jacks cortisol.” then adds an arousal argument beside it. The clipped parallel sentences make two mechanisms sound separate and cumulative. “59% higher odds” supplies numerical urgency after the command, while “is associated” remains the wording of association rather than an explicit causal statement.

**The ending earns a behavioural instruction rather than a sales CTA.** “Replace scrolling with a calming activity: reading, walking, journaling, meditating, etc.” follows “Turn off screens” with things to do in the newly empty hour. It closes the practical gap a prohibition creates. There is no comment keyword, link or follow request, and “Red light glasses are also a good investment” is a recommendation within an earlier item, not a final offer. The post’s completed value is the five cutoffs promised by “These are the 5 cutoffs I followed.”
