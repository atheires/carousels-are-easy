**4**

**9**
**Stop trying to be loved as a whole. You will not be.**

**01** People don't love brands. They obsess over three to five touchpoints inside them.
**02** You cannot know which three. Neither can they, until it happens.
**03** So you engineer for all of them. Twenty-five touchpoints, five in each of the five moments a person meets you: discovery, first contact, purchase, use, return.
**04** Whoever walks in finds the three that were made for them.
**05** Those three are the ones they describe to someone else. They recruit the next customer for you.
**06** Do this and your competitors have no way in. They can copy your product and undercut your price, and it will not matter. You are not running a brand anymore. You are running the cult of your category.

The complete framework publishes soon: the five moments, the twenty-five touchpoints, and how to engineer each one.

## Notes

**Slide 2 is the best single piece of typography in the file.** *"I've been obsessed with this question for years."* — and then *"for years"* is repeated seventeen times down the right edge of an otherwise empty page, ending on a rule and the words `HERE'S MY THESIS`.

It does not *say* the writer is obsessive. It **performs the obsession**, and the empty left two-thirds of the slide gives it room to feel unhinged rather than decorative. **When a claim is about intensity, repetition on the page beats an adjective.** This is the single most transferable visual idea here — and note it costs nothing but nerve.

**Labelling it `AN ESSAY` on slide 1 sets expectations that make ten dense slides acceptable.** The reader agrees to read something long before they start. Compare the vault's 4–9 slide range: **the genre label is what buys permission for length.**

**Slide 3 states the framework and admits it is the author's own product** — *"This is the framework I've developed so any brand can replicate it."* No coyness. The diagram (three filled circles, two empty) makes "three to five" visible in a way the sentence cannot.

**Slide 4 is the proof slide, and its proof is a disagreement.** Three customers, three incompatible reasons, all true. Then the pre-empt: *"That is not inconsistency."* The author names the objection the reader is forming and overrules it in four words — same move as [[22 - Why Doing Everything Right Makes You Forgettable|post 22]]'s *"The customer is not unimpressed. They are unaware."* **This author consistently intercepts the reader's misreading inside the same breath as the claim.**

**"Each person builds their own version of you out of whatever reached them."** The thesis in one sentence, and it reframes brand consistency from *sameness* to *coverage*.

**Slide 9's six numbered points are a chain, not a list.** 01 states the fact, 02 states the problem it creates, 03 gives the response, 04–05 give the consequence, 06 gives the payoff. Read them out of order and they collapse. Compare post 22's slide 9, which was five *conditions* — same visual treatment (rules, `01`–`06`, tiny numerals), different logical structure. **The design is a house template; the reasoning inside it is rebuilt each time.**

**"You are not running a brand anymore. You are running the cult of your category."** The paired-verdict closing line again.

**The CTA sells something that does not exist yet, and says so.** *"The complete framework publishes soon: the five moments, the twenty-five touchpoints, and how to engineer each one."* It names three specific missing pieces — the same technique as post 22's CTA, which named three unanswered questions. **Naming exactly what is withheld makes the gap feel measurable rather than vague.** Note also that "publishes soon" converts a lead magnet into a waiting list, which is why the word is *LOVED* and the promise is *first*.

**Same comment word across both posts.** *LOVED* funnels posts 22 and 27 into one launch — the same one-word-two-posts pattern as Creator College's *"story"* in posts 24 and 25.
