## Notes

**The hook accuses the reader of an influence before naming the influenced belief.** “sorry to break it to you but / you’ve been brainwashed if you think...” combines the social softener with an unusually severe word. “if” and the unfinished clause make the accusation conditional: the reader has to swipe to find out whether it applies. The attack is on what feels necessary or normal, not simply on how much someone spends.

**Slide 2 supplies a familiar calendar rule as the first test.** “you have to buy new clothes for each new season” completes the suspended cover sentence. “have to” is the pressure point; the claim is not that new clothes are inherently wrong. Beginning with seasonal shopping gives “brainwashed” an everyday referent before slide 3's “£250 a month” brings a sharper financial threshold into the argument.

**The recurring clause makes separate purchases look like one learned compulsion.** “you’ve been brainwashed if you think” returns before a gym, a holiday haul, bodycare and trainers. There are no literal questions, but every return asks the reader to test another belief against the same diagnosis. The repeated “need” moves the examples out of taste and into imagined obligation, which is how the list builds an argument rather than remaining a miscellany of expensive things.

**The middle examples identify replacement before exhaustion as the distortion.** “new products before finishing the ones you have” follows “a haul for your holiday” by challenging accumulation that does not even need an occasion. “multiple pairs of trainers for a hobby” then questions whether equipment has outgrown the activity it serves. “before finishing” and “for a hobby” give the criticism concrete limits; neither line requires the reader to reject all bodycare or exercise purchases.

**The sequence shifts from buying objects to obeying an audience.** “you need to hop on every trend” moves beyond a category of goods, then “you’re always expected to have your nails done” names social expectation directly. “always” makes the maintenance endless. Following this with “outfits aren’t meant to be repeated” reveals the same rule applied to clothes already owned: the pressure can devalue possessions without any change in their usefulness.

**The aesthetic line makes identity itself part of the shopping constraint.** “you need to choose and stick to an aesthetic” comes after the outfit-repetition rule, when the reader has already seen appearance become an obligation. “choose and stick” is a paired demand: first classify yourself, then remain consistent. The post broadens from isolated purchases to the idea that a person's everyday self must fit a stable, consumable category.

**The penultimate item changes direction to protect the reader's agency.** “doing your part doesn’t make a difference” breaks the run of beliefs about needing to buy. After being told that many wants are implanted, the reader could conclude individual choices are pointless; this line names that resignation as another distortion. It prepares the last imperative by making resistance feel consequential rather than merely frugal.

**The last line finally names the source and gives the reader a boundary.** “don’t let social media warp your normal” follows ten examples instead of preceding them, so “normal” now has specific content: seasons, monthly fees, unused products, outfits and aesthetics. “warp” carries the force of “brainwashed” without repeating the accusation. This is a behavioural CTA, with no follow, comment or product request; the earned action is to resist the standard the examples have exposed.

**This is an Inventory used as an Argument about manufactured necessity.** “it’s normal to buy” and “you’re always expected” reveal why the items belong together: the reader has mistaken a feed's repeated displays for rules of ordinary life. The main insight is stated most completely at the end, in “your normal.” The deliberately recurring prefix gives the reader ten chances to recognise one particular kind of pressure before that compression lands.
