**3 — 3/9**

LEVEL 1: STORYTELLING  
*The Reporter*

The reporter includes the core facts of the story but sequences them in a super predictable way.

*A retro TV reading:* This happened. then this happened. then this happened.

Level 1 is like newscaster reading off a teleprompter. It's informative but not that engaging. Graduate from this level as soon as possible.

**4 — 4/9**

LEVEL 2: STORYTELLING  
*The Illusionist*

You have the facts. Now add hooks that pull people in. Great hooks don't change the story. They redirect attention.

*Diagram:* AUDIENCE EXPECTATION line crossed by an arrow at STORY PIVOT, pointing to MISDIRECTION.

Make them expect one thing… then take them in a different direction. Do it a few times, and you've got their attention.

**5 — 5/9**

LEVEL 3: STORYTELLING  
*The Champion*

Keep the facts + hooks. Now add stakes. Show the problems, use cases, or characters that make people feel connected to the story.

*A glowing figure at the centre of a web of connected points.*

Without stakes, the story falls flat. No stakes = no emotional connection.

**6 — 6/9**

LEVEL 4: STORYTELLING  
*The Architect*

Now we're entering advanced storytelling. Keep the facts, hooks, and stakes then add arcs and loops.

*A red wave with three dashed circles.*

Loops are mini-subplots that open, build tension, and close.

**7 — 7/9**

LEVEL 5: STORYTELLING  
*The Translator*

Take everything from the first 4 levels then add clarity + comprehension. Make sure your message is 100% clear.

*A grey block with an arrow to a ruled red box.*

Reframe the message. Use better visuals. Add that final layer of polish. That's what takes good storytelling to the next level.

**8 — 8/9**

LEVEL 6: STORYTELLING  
*The Maestro*

This level is about adding your unique fingerprint. Your style. Your voice. Your way of telling stories.

*A head made of a fingerprint.*

You won't have it figured out at first. But over time, you develop it and it becomes the layer that sets you apart.

## Notes

**The cover is a promise with an object.** "How to get people addicted to your storytelling" gives the benefit and the physical ladder gives the structure: the reader sees there are rungs before they know what they are. The ladder returns on every slide as a column of six red dots with one dot filled black, so the reader always knows which rung they are on.

**Each level is built from the one below.** The sentences start with "Keep the facts + hooks. Now add stakes." and "Keep the facts, hooks, and stakes then add arcs and loops." The cumulative wording teaches that the levels stack and makes the skipped step visible: a reader sees that they cannot have loops without stakes.

**Every level has a name that is a person.** The Reporter, The Illusionist, The Champion, The Architect, The Translator, The Maestro. A role is easier to remember than a number, and the reader can place themselves ("I am a Reporter").

**The CTA keeps the framework's name.** "Comment LADDER and I'll send you a full video" and the promise of "real examples, step-by-step tactics, and exactly how to apply each level" name the three things the six slides leave out.
