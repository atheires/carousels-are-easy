# 23 — The Anatomy Of Great Packaging

**URL:** https://www.instagram.com/p/DbgisbclW8x/
**Slides:** 7
**Type:** Argument, sold as a portfolio. Every slide is a different real packaging project.

---

## Slide copy, verbatim

**1 — cover**
**The anatomy of great packaging.**
*Over a close-up of a lemon covered in brightly printed stickers.*

**2**
Most packaging is designed to look nice on a shelf.

Great packaging is designed to change how someone feels about what's inside.

**Looks nice gets picked up. Feels right gets bought.**

**3**
**Ask yourself:**
What should someone believe about your product before they even open it?

Premium?
Trustworthy?
Different from everything next to it?

Your packaging should answer that in three seconds. ⌛

**4**
Break it down and it's never just the design.

It's the weight in their hand.
The material.
The unboxing sequence.
The first line of copy they read.

**Every layer is a decision about how they'll remember you.**

**5**
The brands people photograph and post: They design for the moment after the purchase, not just the moment of it.

Texture over trend.
Story over slogans.
Restraint over clutter.

**6**
**Packaging isn't the box your product comes in. It's the first thing your product ever says to someone.**

**7 — CTA**
Want packaging that sells before anyone reads a word? Let's design that.

**→ Apply through the link in bio.**

## Notes

**Seven slides, seven different client projects, zero case-study framing.** The work is the wallpaper. There is no "here's a project we did" — the argument runs on top of the portfolio, so the reader absorbs the quality of the studio's work while reading about principles. **Proof by accumulation rather than by claim.**

**"Looks nice gets picked up. Feels right gets bought."** Four words against four words, one verb apart. Same two-clause compression as [[22 - Why Doing Everything Right Makes You Forgettable|post 22]]'s *"Once is an anecdote. Every time is a belief."* Both posts state the whole thesis in a sentence a reader can carry.

**Slide 3 hands the reader a question to ask about their own product.** *"What should someone believe about your product before they even open it?"* Then three candidate answers as one-word questions — *Premium? Trustworthy? Different from everything next to it?* — so the reader starts answering rather than reading. **Making the reader do the work mid-post is what converts a browse into a consultation.**

**The three-second test** mirrors [[16 - Your Carousel Is Dying On The Cover Slide|post 16]]'s half-second test. Both give the reader a stopwatch. A rule with a number attached gets applied; a rule without one gets nodded at.

**Slide 4 lists the invisible layers.** *"It's the weight in their hand. The material. The unboxing sequence. The first line of copy they read."* Four fragments, deliberately physical, moving from touch to text. Then the summarising line: *"Every layer is a decision about how they'll remember you."* Note this is the same underlying idea as post 22 — memory as the real product — arrived at from a different direction.

**"They design for the moment after the purchase, not just the moment of it."** The reframe of the post. Most of the audience optimises the shelf; this moves the target to the customer's kitchen table and their camera roll.

**"Texture over trend. Story over slogans. Restraint over clutter."** Three X-over-Y pairs in a row. Compressed, memorable, and each one quietly disqualifies a competitor's approach.

**Slide 6 is the thesis, alone, in the largest type in the post, over the most striking image** — a cheap red plastic takeaway bag held in a hand against a red ground. The humblest possible packaging illustrating the grandest claim. *"Packaging isn't the box your product comes in. It's the first thing your product ever says to someone."*

**Choosing the low-status example for the biggest claim** is the move worth stealing. A luxury box would have made the line obvious; the plastic bag makes it a revelation.

**The CTA is a qualifying ask, not a lead magnet.** *"Want packaging that sells before anyone reads a word? Let's design that. → Apply through the link in bio."* The word **Apply** does the filtering — it presumes selection, which suits a studio selling projects rather than a creator selling a community. Note it echoes the thesis: *sells before anyone reads a word.*
