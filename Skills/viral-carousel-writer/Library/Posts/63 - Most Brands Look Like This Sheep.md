## Notes

**The cover makes the branding problem visible before explaining it.** *“Most brands look like this sheep.”* points to an image that is itself made to resemble the familiar flock, so the reader gets a visual joke and a claim at once. The odd specificity of “this sheep” creates a reason to swipe: the next slides will explain what the sheep stands for.

**Slide 2 names the contradiction behind the image.** *“Everyone wants to stand out, but nobody wants to look too different.”* follows the sheep comparison by giving it a human motive. The two halves pull in opposite directions, making conformity feel less like a design accident and more like a choice people make while trying to stay safe.

**Slide 3 shows the outcome of that contradiction.** *“different brands wearing the same colours, fonts & visual language”* turns the abstract wish to fit in into recognizable design ingredients. The repeated sheep now make sense: surface variety can still produce one visual herd.

**Slide 4 shifts from sameness to its consequence.** *“even a good brand can get lost in the herd”* follows the list of shared visual cues by explaining the cost: competent work is not enough when it blends into the category. “Herd” carries the cover image forward, so the metaphor binds the argument rather than decorating it.

**Slide 5 sharpens the diagnosis by separating quality from distinction.** *“The problem isn’t that your branding looks bad. It’s that it looks familiar.”* answers the likely objection that the work is already polished. That clarification follows the consequence on slide 4 and makes the real issue harder to dismiss: the brand may look good and still be forgettable.

**Slide 6 states the practical test in one sentence.** *“Your brand should be recognisable before anyone has to read the name.”* gives a positive standard after the previous slides have defined what fails. It moves the reader from noticing visual sameness to imagining a brand whose identity is legible without its label.

**Slide 7 makes the solution a category-level ambition.** *“It’s about making the category.”* completes the contrast with *“fitting into the category”* from the same slide. This is the argument’s biggest step: once recognition is the test, distinctiveness can shape what customers expect from the whole space.

**The CTA keeps the sheep metaphor alive while making a clear offer.** *“Break the bhe[d] cha[a]l.”* is a visual wordplay on “herd” and the flock imagery; *“Build a brand that looks like nobody else”* translates the joke into a service promise. The closing line *“Branding that refuses to follow the herd”* echoes slide 4, giving the final frame a memorable phrase that belongs to this specific argument.

**This is an argument carousel with a visual metaphor doing structural work.** The sheep begins as a joke, reappears as the consequence of sameness, and returns in the CTA as the thing the brand can escape. Each slide advances the claim from shared desire, to shared style, to lost recognition, and finally to category-making.
