## Notes

**The cover makes a strong ranking claim before it reveals the criteria.** *“5 of these AI agents are a waste of money”* names a field of six options, while *“only 1 is actually worth paying”* promises a clear verdict. Showing the product names around the headline previews the comparison set and makes the swipe feel like a route to the promised decision.

**Slide 2 begins with a compact product profile, then balances strengths against a specific friction.** *“An agent inside your ChatGPT workflow”* defines Dots before the pros and cons. The positive list emphasizes familiarity and integrations, then *“One thread gets messy”* names a cost that follows directly from the workflow description. Price and “WHO IT'S FOR” turn the review into a decision aid.

**Slide 3 shifts from one assistant to a coordinated team.** *“A team for repeat business tasks”* gives Grok Bot a different use case from the prior slide's single ChatGPT workflow. The line *“Bots delegate work to each other”* supports the team framing, while *“Pricey for the results”* immediately tests that promise against cost and supervision.

**Slide 4 interrupts the comparison with a useful CTA and a buying principle.** *“COMMENT ‘AGENTS’ to get a full guide on all AI agents”* offers a deeper resource before the remaining products appear. The final warning—*“Pick the job first, then the agent”*—also gives readers a criterion they can apply to every option, so the interruption advances the buying decision rather than pausing it.

**The sequence resumes with products grouped by the reader's context and tolerance for setup.** Muse is described as *“Your Instagram + Facebook are easily connected,”* while Hermes is *“Your agent. Your models. Your setup.”* Those lines make the intended user legible before the detailed pros and cons: social creators for Muse, technically confident users for Hermes.

**The small wording choices make risk feel concrete.** Muse's advantage is *“Feels like WhatsApp,”* followed by the caution *“Needs babysitting.”* Instinct's friendly *“Feels like a PA”* is answered by *“Hard to see inside,”* then a specific concern about default data training. The parenthetical *“(sus)”* gives the privacy critique a casual voice without hiding the underlying issue.

**The verdict arrives after the alternatives have been made legible.** *“Winner: ChatGPT Dots”* is supported by *“Best value for money”* and an explicit personal reason: *“Dots already fit inside my daily workflow. I don't have to pay extra.”* The “WHEN I'D SWITCH” section qualifies the recommendation with two use-case exceptions, making the preference more credible.

**The final product card comes after the winner, so it reads as a final exception rather than a delayed contender.** Manus Cue's *“Agents with their own phone, email + wallet”* is a distinctive proposition, but *“Invite only for now”* and *“Has not been tested thoroughly”* give the reader reasons to wait. Its position lets the carousel close the comparison with limits rather than reopening the ranking.

**This is an Inventory carousel organized around a purchase decision.** Each agent gets a consistent set of attributes—description, pros, cons, price, and intended user—while the CTA and winner slide supply the selection logic. The comparison stays useful because it moves from broad coverage to a personal recommendation with clear exceptions.
