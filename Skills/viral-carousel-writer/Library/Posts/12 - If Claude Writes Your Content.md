## Slide copy, verbatim

**1 — cover**
**If Claude writes your content, *why* on earth would I wire you $18k to access your thinking?**

**2**
Since everyone's using AI for content creation and contributing to the *mass recycling* of the same shit, selling high-ticket from content has never been easier.

**3**
When I audit my clients' content I can spot within 30 seconds whether they wrote it or their GPT / Claude project.

*(Especially when I work with clients long term and know their personality on a deeper level)*

**4**
By the way:
**I LOVE AI.**

I'm currently rebuilding my own AI tool and use it every day to help me execute certain things faster. For structures and system it's fab.

**5**
But for *creative* content creation??

It doest the WORST thing that can happen to a business owner which is: lack of self-trust. You become addicted to needing validation and we all know what that does to your sales.

**6**
The rise of AI made you stop trusting your own *voice*, opinions and capabilities.

And the deeper reason is that you don't want to say the "wrong" thing or be perceived as less capable or be misunderstood. So you hyper-control the way you're perceived.

**7**
When you do this your 10/10 post becomes a 6/10 because Claude removed the little imperfections & rawness of your actual voice.

**8**
And sububconsciously here's what happens: your dream client *processes* your content but she doesn't *experience* it.

**9**
And that distinction matters MASSIVELY when you're selling high-ticket.

Wanna know why?? (You should know by now)
Because she's not paying $10K, $20K or $50K for fucking INFORMATION!!!!

**10**
She's paying for the way YOU see things, your *lived* experience and your fck-ups.

She's paying for the (human) brain she wants access to because it makes her see something she couldn't see on her own.

**11**
I love you because the fact that you're using AI for content tells me you *care*.

You want to do a great job. You want your content to sound elevated and position you as the authority you are, i totally get that!

**12**
But right now the *opposite* is actually happening without you even noticing it.

Your dream client reads your posts, saves it and then buys from the person she can actually experience and feels connected to.

## Notes

**The hook is a question the reader cannot answer.** *"If Claude writes your content, why on earth would I wire you $18k to access your thinking?"* It is aimed directly at the reader's business model, uses a specific dollar figure, and has no comfortable answer. Notice it is framed from the *buyer's* side — "why would **I** wire **you**" — so the reader is forced to stand where their own client stands.

**Slide 4 is the concession slide and it is load-bearing.** *"By the way: I LOVE AI."* Placed early, in the largest type, unqualified. Without it, everything after slide 3 reads as an anti-AI rant and half the audience leaves. With it, the argument becomes *"I use this daily and here is where it breaks"*, which is much harder to dismiss. **When you attack something the audience uses, concede your own use of it before you press the attack.**

**The argument descends one layer at a time, and each slide is one link.**

> everyone uses AI (2) → I can spot it in 30 seconds (3) → but I love AI (4) → not for creative work (5) → because it kills self-trust (6) → so your 10/10 becomes a 6/10 (7) → so she *processes* instead of *experiences* (8) → which matters at high ticket (9) → because she's buying your brain, not information (10) → and I know you meant well (11) → but the opposite is happening (12)

No slide skips a rung and no slide carries two. **Twelve slides is only tolerable because each one is a single move.**

**`processes` vs `experiences` is the whole post in two words.** One italicised word against another. That contrast is set up on slide 8, justified on 9–10, and paid off in the last line of slide 12 — *"buys from the person she can actually experience."* **A carousel this long needs one two-word distinction that the reader can carry out with them.**

**It ends on the diagnosis, not on advice.** There is no "so here's what to do", no comment word, no offer. The last slide describes the reader's current situation and stops. For a consultant selling high-ticket thinking, leaving the reader with an unresolved problem *is* the sales mechanism — the missing step is the call.

**Slide 11 does the empathy turn immediately before the wound.** *"I love you because the fact that you're using AI for content tells me you care."* After nine slides of criticism, one slide of genuine credit, then the closing blow. Placing the kindness *second to last* is what stops the whole thing reading as contempt.

**The voice is unpolished on purpose, and the post is about exactly that.** Typos left in — *"doest"*, *"sububconsciously"*, lowercase *"i totally get that!"* — alongside swearing and quadruple exclamation marks. A carousel arguing that AI sands off "the little imperfections & rawness of your actual voice" would be self-refuting if it were clean. Same principle as post 08: **the artefact has to demonstrate its own claim.**

**Typographic restraint carries the seriousness.** Brown leather texture, one elegant serif, italics as the only emphasis, a small brand mark top and bottom. No arrows, no highlights, no screenshots. The look says "an essay", which is what it is.

**The two-tier text pattern is used on almost every slide**: a large serif assertion, a rule, then small-set explanation underneath. The big line is the claim; the small line is the reasoning. A reader can swipe the whole post reading only the big lines and still get the argument — which is exactly how carousels are actually read.
