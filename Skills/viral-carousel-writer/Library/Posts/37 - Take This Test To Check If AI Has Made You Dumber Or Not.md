## Notes

**“Dumber” makes an abstract concern personally insulting enough to test.** “Take This Test / to Check If / AI Has Made You / Dumber or Not” attacks the reader's confidence in their own thinking. “Don’t cheat. Take your time.” immediately changes the reader's role from spectator to participant. The post has asked them to prove something about themselves before it supplies its argument.

**Slide 2 makes the reader experience empty fluency before naming it.** “Which sentence is actually making a point?” comes before the explanation that A and C “sound right, but they barely say anything.” The reader must compare “an increasingly evolving world” with “writing emails by 25% / in one experiment.” The contrast is between impressive motion without an observable event and an event with a bounded result; choosing makes the subsequent criticism feel discovered rather than imposed.

**The memory question makes the swipe itself part of the test.** “Read this ONCE. / Don’t go back.” is followed by “Without going back... / What was the actual finding?” The platform action removes the source from sight just when recall becomes necessary. Only after the reader tries does “reading without / really remembering” name the failure the exercise was arranged to expose.

**The question chain changes the kind of independence being tested.** “Which sentence,” “What was the actual finding,” “What comes to your mind,” and “How would you reply” move from recognising specificity to recalling, questioning, and composing. The missed-deadline message is especially pointed after the research claim: now the reader cannot pass merely by being good at spotting academic wording. They must supply a response to a situation whose facts belong to them.

**The unsupported statistic is a trap with a delayed explanation.** “Studies show that / people who use AI / every day have 40% / worse memory” arrives after a passage that already sounds like a research finding. “Which studies?” and “Where did 40% come from?” do not appear until slide 12. That delay tests whether the reader will challenge a confident number without being told to. These are the carousel's test words, not evidence that the statistic is true.

**The supplied scoring order is untidy, but the answer must not be tidied into it.** “FINAL SCORE” appears on slide 10 before the “different endings” task and before the answer to the 40% question. It lists four-point outcomes although slide 13 awards another point. This interrupts the otherwise clear question–answer rhythm. The late “This obviously / isn’t a real / intelligence test” puts the usable insight elsewhere: “how quickly / you now outsource thinking,” rather than a reliable measured score.

**Small verbal restraints stop the test from becoming an absolute diagnosis.** “you’re probably getting too used,” “you may be outsourcing,” and “that could be a sign” follow tasks that might embarrass the reader. Those qualifiers matter because “Dumber” was such a forceful opening. The repeated “you get 1 point” supplies an easy beat, while “actually,” “specific,” and “on your own” keep redirecting attention from fluent output toward the reader's own mental work.

**This is a Diagnostic / quiz carousel, and the CTA cashes in the reader's completed work.** “Comment / how many / did you get?” asks for a result the reader has already been tracking. The final distinction—“isn’t a real / intelligence test” but a test of outsourcing—also gives a low-scoring reader a less humiliating way to interpret participation. The engagement request is earned by involvement, even though the scoring sequence makes the number itself ambiguous.
