## Notes

**The structure is a loop, and the loop is the whole design.** Slide 1 shows the finished dashboard. Slides 2–7 build it piece by piece. Slide 8 shows *the identical screenshot from slide 1* next to the ask. The reader ends exactly where they started, except now they know what it took — so the same image that was an intriguing object on slide 1 is a wanted object on slide 8. **Opening and closing on the same image turns a hook into a CTA at zero extra cost.**

**Every middle slide is: title → screenshot of that page working → the prompt that made it.** Claim, proof, method. Same three beats, five times. The screenshot always sits *between* the promise and the prompt, so you see it exists before you are asked to believe the prompt produced it.

**The screenshots are of a real account with real numbers** — 355.9K impressions in the sidebar, 36 posts, 324.4k reach, 9.1% engagement, tracked competitors with follower counts. Nothing is a mockup. The specificity of ugly, non-round numbers is what makes it credible.

**Slide 2 is the only slide that is pure setup, and it earns its place** by establishing the scaffold every later prompt plugs into — it names all five sections up front. The reader gets a map before the tour. It is also the only slide with a handwritten "Send this to Claude" instruction; once the pattern is set, it is never repeated.

**`[NICHE]` on slide 7 is left as a literal placeholder.** He didn't fill it in with his own niche. That one bracketed word tells every reader the prompt is theirs to use, not a demo of his setup.

**The caption is three lines and does the work of ten.** A claim with a time-box (*"in a week"*), a list of five deliverables as bare fragments separated by full stops (*"analytics. calendar. competitor tracking. news feed. instagram manager. all of it."*), and the ask. Note that the staccato fragment style — the thing that was wrong in your carousel copy — works fine *in a caption*, where it reads as an index rather than as speech.

**14,913 comments.** The reason is the ratio of effort: the post makes a complete, complex system feel like six paragraphs of typing, and then offers to hand over the six paragraphs. The bigger the gap between what was built and how little it took, the more valuable the shortcut looks.
