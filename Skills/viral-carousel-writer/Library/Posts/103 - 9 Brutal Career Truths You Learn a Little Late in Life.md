Your degree gets you started. It doesn't decide where you end up.

Your career will probably take you places your younger self never planned for. That's okay.

**4 — page 3**

Not every job has to be your dream job.

Sometimes a job is a stepping stone, a learning experience, or simply how you pay the bills. It doesn't have to become your identity.

**5 — page 4**

Being busy doesn't automatically mean you're doing well.

You can spend an entire day answering messages and still not move anything important forward.

**6 — page 5**

"I don't know" is a perfectly valid answer.

Especially when followed by, "but I'll find out."

Knowing how to learn is more useful than pretending you already know everything.

**7 — page 6**

Your first few years aren't a race.

Someone will get promoted before you. Someone will earn more. Someone will figure out their career earlier. Their timeline isn't a deadline for yours.

**8 — page 7**

The people you work with can matter just as much as the work itself.

A great role with the wrong environment can drain you. A decent role with good people can teach you more than you expected.

Sometimes growth looks like leaving.

Not every difficult situation needs to be endured just because it makes you "stronger." Sometimes the lesson is knowing when it's time to move on.

You are allowed to change your mind about what you want.

The career you wanted at 21 doesn't have to be the career you want at 25.

Changing direction isn't automatically falling behind.

## Notes

**The cover promises honesty and a cat apologises for it.** "9 Brutal Career Truths" would sound harsh, so the cat illustration with "Just being real!" and "Not trying to scare you, promise!" softens it. The tone is a kind friend telling the truth, and the rest of the post keeps that voice.

**Every slide is one serif statement and a short sans-serif comfort.** The statement is a blunt line in large serif type ("Being busy doesn't automatically mean you're doing well."), the explanation is two or three plain sentences in smaller type. The reader can take the post as nine headlines or as nine small paragraphs.

**The truths are permissions as much as warnings.** "You are allowed to change your mind." "'I don't know' is a perfectly valid answer." "Your first few years aren't a race." Half the slides tell the reader they are not behind, which is why the post is shareable to a friend.

**The close pivots to the author's own subject with no bridge.** After nine career lessons, the last slide says "Building a personal brand isn't about becoming popular... Start building your brand today." and lists three profile links. The ask is a follow, and the change of topic is abrupt. Compare [[27 - Why We Love The Brands We Love]] for a post that ties its subject to the sell more tightly.
