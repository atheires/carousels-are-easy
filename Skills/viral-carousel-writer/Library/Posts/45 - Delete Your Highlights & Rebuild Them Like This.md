## Notes

**The cover attacks a neglected asset with an unnecessarily decisive verb.** “DELETE YOUR HIGHLIGHTS & REBUILD THEM LIKE THIS” makes a small profile-maintenance task feel like a correction worth doing now. “DELETE” implies the existing arrangement is costing the reader something, while “REBUILD” immediately offers recovery. “LIKE THIS” withholds the replacement just long enough to require a swipe; the cover does not ask the reader to accept destruction without promising an alternative.

**Slide 2 makes the harsh command about visitors rather than aesthetics.** “The right Highlights help build trust and connection with new visitors” supplies the reason the cover omitted. The sentence ends with “follow you and buy from you,” moving from familiarity to two business outcomes. “Here are the ones you need and what to post in each...” then promises both category selection and actual contents. Without that second promise, six Highlight names would leave the rebuilding work with the reader.

**The main insight arrives before the inventory: Highlights are a visitor’s decision aid.** “Make them more likely to follow you and buy from you” changes what the reader thinks they are organising. These are not just archived Stories; they answer whether a stranger likes the person, understands the offer and believes the claims. “About me” comes first because “see if you're their kind of person” is a lower-pressure entry than being sold a service immediately.

**The question chain is embedded inside the Offers section.** “what is it? who is it for? how does it work? what problem does it solve? how to get started or buy?” moves from identification to relevance, explanation, value and action. It comes after “Show what you sell,” turning that vague instruction into the questions a visitor needs answered. “Why it's worth it” is therefore demonstrated through coverage, not left as a demand to write more persuasive sales copy.

**BTS and Social Proof answer different doubts in that order.** “Makes your brand feel more real and approachable” answers the distance created by an offer; “Makes it easier for people to trust you” then answers whether the work delivers. “Snippets from your workdays” are evidence of a working person, while “case studies” and “‘before vs after’ photos” are evidence of an outcome. The separation keeps friendliness from being treated as proof of competence.

**The final core sections turn usefulness into care, then care into familiarity.** “Shows you actually care about their experience” makes FAQs an interpersonal gesture after the reviews have done the credibility work. “Life” follows with “your life outside of work,” so the personal material is additional connection rather than the only reason to follow. The early “About me” introduces the professional identity; the late “Life” expands a person the visitor already understands.

**The naming suggestions finish the implementation at the level where beginners stall.** “meet me, about me, my story” and “reviews, wins, love notes” give the reader words they can place on their profile immediately. “Love notes” softens a sales term without changing its purpose. After six core sections, “A FEW MORE IDEAS” shifts to conditional language — “If you host,” “If you have,” “If you've been featured” — so optional evidence is not mistaken for a universal requirement.

**The CTA scales the free decision aid into personalised strategy.** “A detailed, step-by-step plan personalized for your business” follows a post that has already delivered a small, usable plan. “Growth,” “engagement” and “sales” repeat the follow-and-buy rationale of slide 2, so the offer continues the stated goal. This is an **Inventory with implementation detail**: “what to post in each” remains the organising promise, and the repeated contents and name ideas make each item usable. The paid value is broader personalisation, not access to the six names already given away.
