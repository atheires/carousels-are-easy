## Notes

**The cover narrows a broad topic to a first action.** *“How to List Your First Digital Product Online”* promises a beginner-level process, and *“Beginner Friendly”* removes the worry that the reader needs prior platform knowledge. The phrase *“your first”* makes the task feel finite and personal, setting up a step-by-step Build rather than a general business lecture.

**Slide 2 turns the promise into a decision before asking for production work.** *“CHOOSE YOUR ONLINE SHOP”* comes first because the platform determines where the listing will live. Its note explains the practical criteria—upload, product page, payment, automatic delivery—then names Raketph, Etsy, and Gumroad as options. The reader gets a reason to choose, not just a menu of logos.

**Slide 3 answers the buyer’s visualization problem.** *“Don’t just upload a screenshot of your design!”* identifies a weak listing, then *“Create simple mockups to help customers visualize what they’re getting”* gives the replacement and its purpose. The next sentence specifies what the mockup should reveal: cover, inside pages, inclusions, and use. The birthday invitation examples make that advice tangible without pausing for a separate case study.

**Slide 4 moves from presentation to the information needed to buy.** *“ADD YOUR PRODUCT DETAILS”* follows the mockup advice because attractive visuals still need a complete offer. The listing note names title, description, contents, price, and files, then asks the creator to answer likely buyer questions. *“PUBLISH!”* supplies a clear finish line to this setup stage.

**Slide 5 corrects the assumption that publishing creates discovery.** *“DON'T JUST LIST IT MARKET IT!”* is a sharper pivot than another setup instruction. The copy explains the gap directly: *“people need to discover your product before they can buy it.”* Behind-the-scenes content, previews, tutorials, and demonstrations give the reader specific ways to address that gap, while the sales figures pictured beside the account profile act as proof of a working shop.

**The slide order forms a practical dependency chain.** *“Choose your online shop”* establishes the destination; *“Create your product mockups”* shows the offer; *“Add your product details”* completes the listing; *“Don’t just list it market it!”* extends the work to promotion. Each step resolves the next obstacle in putting a digital product online, so the carousel behaves like a compact beginner’s checklist.

**The CTA is earned by the beginner framing but switches from listing to learning.** *“if you’re a beginner who wants to learn more about Canva + digital products...”* qualifies the audience, then *“Comment ‘START’ and I’ll share my beginner’s guide”* gives a low-friction next move. It follows naturally from the how-to sequence because the post has shown the shape of the work and the guide offers continued help, though the promised resource broadens beyond listing alone.

**This is a Build carousel with a lead-generation close.** The numbered steps make the genre explicit, and the advice progresses in execution order instead of collecting unrelated tips. Unlike an argument driven by a question chain, its engine is sequence: choose a sales home, prepare the visuals, complete the listing, then market it. The final comment prompt converts that instructional value into a measurable response.
