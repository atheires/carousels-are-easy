## Slide copy, verbatim

**1 — cover**
**Write the way you speak**

If you’d normally say:
*“this stressed me out SOOOOO bad”*

Don’t suddenly write:
*“This experience created significant emotional distress”*

If your real personality sounds chaotic and slightly unhinged,
let THAT version show up in your writing too!!

The more natural your writing sounds, the easier it is for people to trust there’s a REAL person behind it.

**2**
**Replace vague with visual.**

VAGUE | VISUAL

Feeling anxious → Refreshing the tracking link every 6 minutes

Busy morning → Answering emails with one eye open while downing my second coffee at 7AM

Bad date → Realizing halfway that I was carrying the entire conversation on my back

Visual writing helps people picture the moment instead of just understanding it logically
(and the brain LOVES that!!)

**3**
**Mix up your sentence length.**

2 reasons.

One, you’re not AI.

Two, human conversation naturally changes rhythm. Some thoughts are short. Some pause dramatically. for... wait for it... emotional damage. Some ramble a little because your brain suddenly remembered 14 other stuff halfway through the sentence (like what I’m doing now).

**4**
**Add inside jokes, references, reactions, and pauses.**

“girl math told me this purchase was basically free” (inside joke)

“You can’t sit with us.” (reference IYKYK)

“HELLO??? 😭” (reaction)

“wait for it…” (pause)

These tiny personalities are what make writing feel ALIVE instead of sounding like it was generated in a beige corporate void

**5**
**Don’t overthink it.**

No, really. Write crap. Write bad.
Write things you’ll cringe at later.

Some of the best lines ever written sound slightly imperfect, oddly specific, and a little emotionally unhinged… and that’s exactly what makes it human.

## Notes

**The cover opens with a voice instruction, then proves the voice in the same frame.** *“Write the way you speak”* is immediately followed by the contrast between *“this stressed me out SOOOOO bad”* and *“This experience created significant emotional distress.”* The examples make the abstract advice audible before the reader swipes; the deliberately stretched “SOOOOO” is already doing the thing it recommends.

**Slide 2 turns the instruction into a before-and-after the reader can picture.** *“Feeling anxious → Refreshing the tracking link every 6 minutes”* replaces a named feeling with the tiny behavior that reveals it. The previous slide asks the reader to sound like a person; this one shows how a specific, slightly exposing detail makes a person visible.

**Slide 3 changes the scale from word choice to sentence movement.** *“Some thoughts are short. Some pause dramatically. for... wait for it... emotional damage.”* follows the visual examples by explaining the rhythm behind them, then performs that rhythm through the abrupt fragments and intentionally awkward lowercase “for.” The aside *“like what I’m doing now”* lets the slide demonstrate its own point.

**Slide 4 gives the reader small devices for making that voice recognizable.** The list of *“inside jokes, references, reactions, and pauses”* is followed by one example of each, including *“HELLO??? 😭”*. This comes after sentence rhythm because it adds social texture to the voice; the final line names the contrast as writing that feels *“ALIVE”* instead of corporate.

**Slide 5 removes the pressure to get the voice right on the first try.** *“No, really. Write crap. Write bad.”* is a blunt, comic reset after four slides of craft advice. Its short commands enact the looseness being defended, while *“slightly imperfect, oddly specific”* collects the earlier examples into a compact standard for human-sounding lines.

**The sales turn grows out of the problem the carousel has been naming.** Slide 6 says *“If you’re DONE with writing that sounds like it graduated from AI Slop University...”* and introduces Sofia as a copywriter and mentor. The offer to write websites, sales pages, and launch copy follows naturally because the preceding slides have established the desired voice and the reader’s frustration with its opposite; *“DM ME ‘COPY’”* gives that frustration a direct next step.

**The follow prompt offers more of the same voice-led material before the branded loop closes.** *“for more writing and storytelling rabbitholes”* is a relaxed promise rather than a new benefit claim. Slide 8 then supplies *“How to write like a human in the age of AI slop”* as the closing frame video, restating the broader subject after the specific five-point advice and keeping the loop tied to the carousel’s opening idea.

**The carousel is an argument delivered as a short build.** It begins with a claim about natural voice, moves through visual detail, rhythm, and personality, then lowers the stakes with permission to draft badly. The CTA sells help with the exact problem those examples have made concrete, instead of interrupting the teaching with an unrelated pitch.
