## Slide copy, verbatim

**1 — cover**
*Window chrome, `SECOND_BRAIN.sys`, a stack of folders with a cursor on it:*
**3 Folders**
*raw + wiki + outputs*

**I Built My Second Brain With 3 Folders**

It's not an app. It's not a plugin.
It's just **3 folders** on your computer.

**2**
**01. Three Folders.**

```
$ mkdir raw/
$ mkdir wiki/
$ mkdir outputs/
```

*That's the entire architecture.*

- `raw/` — Your junk drawer of notes, highlights, and saved articles
- `wiki/` — Where the AI writes clean, organized knowledge pages
- `outputs/` — Answers and drafts the AI generates for you

→ **But folders alone won't work without this**

**3**
**02. The Schema File**

Create one file called **CLAUDE.md** in your project root. It tells the AI **what your knowledge base is about** and how to organize it.

> **# Knowledge Base Schema**
> **## What This Is**
> A personal knowledge base about [YOUR TOPIC].
> **## How It's Organized**
> `raw/` contains unprocessed source material. Never modify these files.
> `wiki/` contains the organized wiki. AI maintains this entirely.
> `outputs/` contains generated reports, answers, and analyses.
> **## Wiki Rules**
> Every topic gets its own .md file in wiki/
> Every wiki file starts with a one-paragraph summary
> Link related topics using [[topic-name]] format
> Maintain an INDEX.md that lists every topic
> When new raw sources are added, update wiki articles
> **## My Interests**
> [List 3-5 things you want this KB to focus on]

→ **Now you need to feed it**

**4**
**03. Dump Everything**

1. **Drop anything into raw/** — Bookmarks, screenshots, notes, transcripts
2. **Export notes from any app** — Apple Notes, Notion, Google Docs. All of it.

> **Don't organize it. Don't rename files.**
> **The AI's job is to sort the mess. Your job is to dump it.**

→ **Here's exactly what to say**

**5**
**04. Tell The AI To Build It**

```
Read everything in raw/. Compile a wiki
following the rules in CLAUDE.md.
```

One prompt. Your entire knowledge base **organized.**

→ **Then the real power kicks in**

**6**
**05. Ask It Anything**

- *"What are the gaps in my understanding of X?"*
- *"Compare what source A says vs source B"*
- *"Write me a briefing on X using only what's in here"*

**ASK → SAVE → SMARTER**

Every answer gets saved back into the wiki.
The next question builds on the last.
**That's the compounding loop.**

→ **But who is this actually for?**

**7**
**06. This Works For Everyone**

*If you're consuming it, you should be compounding it.*

- **Content Creators** — Which topics, hooks, and formats actually perform. Query your own data.
- **Business Owners** — Every meeting, decision, and client detail. One place, forever searchable.
- **Researchers** — 50 papers synthesized into one wiki. Ask questions across all of them at once.
- **Students** — Build the wiki a textbook never gave you. Personalized, connected, yours.

→ **One more thing to keep it clean**

**8**
**07. Monthly Health Check**

**Scan wiki → Flag errors → Auto-fix**

> THE PROMPT
> *"Review the entire wiki. Flag contradictions. Find unsourced claims. List dead ends. Fix what you can. Flag the rest."*

Once a month, run one prompt. The AI audits everything.
**Contradictions, unsourced claims, dead ends.**
It fixes what it can. Flags the rest.

→ **Save this before you forget it**

Not one of them is a summary. Every one is **a question or a promise the reader now wants resolved**, and the next slide resolves it and opens the next. The chain is unbroken from slide 2 to slide 9. This is what "every slide creates a question, the next slide answers it" looks like when someone actually commits to it — and it is worth noting they *wrote the connective tissue out loud* rather than trusting the reader to feel it.

**The last one is the cleverest.** *"Save this before you forget it →"* is not a content promise at all — it is an instruction to save, disguised as a chain link, placed one slide before the ask. It boosts the save metric and delivers the reader to the CTA already in a compliant frame.

**The hook's power is subtraction.** *"It's not an app. It's not a plugin. It's just 3 folders on your computer."* Two negations then the reveal. The claim is not "this is powerful" but "this is smaller than you think" — **the NESB lever being pulled is Easy, hard, and nothing else.** The number 3 appears in the headline, in the folder graphic, and in the subhead.

**Slide 2 makes the whole system fit in three shell commands** and then says *"That's the entire architecture."* Showing the complete thing on the second slide is a risk that pays off — it proves the "just 3 folders" claim immediately, and the remaining six slides become about *making it work* rather than about what it is.

**Numbered steps 01–07 in the slide titles**, plus a square progress bar at the bottom of every slide that fills in as you swipe. The reader always knows how much is left. Compare post 08, which did the same job with a running 01–42 count.

**Slide 7 is the "who is this for" slide, placed second-to-last.** Four audiences, one line each, each written as the outcome *that* person wants. It is a widening move right before the ask — anyone who had not yet placed themselves in the post gets a chance to. Note the italic line above it: *"If you're consuming it, you should be compounding it."*

**The comment word is `'Brain'`** — rendered in a monospace code box with a cursor clicking it, matching the file-system aesthetic of the whole post. And the deliverable is named as three specific artefacts: *"Schema file. Folder structure. The exact prompt that builds it."* Not "a guide".
