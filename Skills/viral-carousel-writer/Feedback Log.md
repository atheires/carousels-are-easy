# Feedback Log

Every correction the original author gave this writer, and every mistake the writer found in its own work. **Read this before writing any carousel and before re-drafting after a critique.** Repeating an entry is the worst failure this skill can have. Newest entries at the bottom.

**How to use it:** after drafting, re-read the RULE line of every entry and check the draft against it. After any correction (his, or one found in a critique), append a new entry in the same turn, before replying. If an earlier entry already covers the lesson, add the new instance under it and strengthen its check instead of duplicating. File a correction by what it is about, never by where it happened: a rule about a sentence shape governs every sentence.

**Entry format:**
```
## F000. Date. Short title
**What I wrote:** the line or move
**What he said:** his exact words, or "Found in critique: …"
**RULE:** what to do or never do
**Why it fails:** the mechanism
**Check:** how to detect it in a draft
**Library evidence:** post numbers that show the right way
```

## F001. 16th September 2026. A "you're not X, you're Y" cover is banned

**What I wrote:** cover, "You're not bad at prompting. You're just starting over."
**What he said:** "i want the cover to be changed I don't like it... 'you're not this, you're this' shouldn't be a carousel cover ever."
**RULE:** Never build a cover on a negation followed by a correction. Banned family: "You're not X, you're Y"; "It's not X, it's Y"; "You don't have an X problem, you have a Y problem"; "The problem isn't X, it's Y."
**Why it fails:** it is the most-used cover construction in the niche, so it reads as a copywriter's reflex rather than a real thought; it tells the reader what they are before giving them anything; and the reversal is the insight, so the cover spends the idea instead of creating a reason to swipe.
**Check:** search the cover for "not … but/, it's/, you're" shapes.
**Library evidence:** the winners' covers are plain (104, 95, 81, 106). Instead use a concrete action, a countable object, a plain "how to", or a plain question.

## F002. 16th September 2026. The cover must be understandable at a glance; direct beats clever

**What I wrote:** "The 8 sentences you've retyped 200 times."
**What he said:** "I don't like it, look at the references you analyzed and see how they do this, I feel like the cover isn't understandable of what you're actually talking about." And on 16th September about "6 Things I Eat for Faster Hair Growth": "I don't understand after reading what you've on the cover what you're talking about... easy and direct beats clever and clumsy."
**RULE:** The cover names its subject in plain words. If a stranger cannot say what the post is about in two seconds without decoding an image, a number or a turn of phrase, it is wrong, however well it scores on curiosity. Default to a plain benefit line: number or limit, what it is, the result. Only consider an oblique, artefact-led or ritual-led cover after the direct version has been written and judged weaker.
**Why it fails:** I hide the SUBJECT behind an image or a count and call the resulting fog curiosity. Withholding the subject is confusion; tension comes from the body paying off a clear promise.
**Check:** read the cover cold. Can it finish "this is about…"?
**Library evidence:** "MASTER COLOUR THEORY", "How to generate endless content ideas", "How to raise your rates without losing clients", "I SPENT 1000 HOURS VIBE CODING (here's everything I learned)", "Freelancing 101 (How to become a freelancer in 8 weeks)". A parenthetical kicker that kills the effort objection ("+FREE Templates at the end", "(in 8 weeks)") is a proven bonus.

## F003. 16th September 2026. The banned X/Y shape applies to every slide, not just covers

**What I wrote:** CTA slide headline, "You already have the file. You just haven't written it." One turn after logging F001.
**What he said:** "...especially when it uses the it's not X it's Y."
**RULE:** F001 is a line-level rule. No negation-then-correction anywhere: cover, body, nuance slide, CTA, caption. Scan every headline in the finished draft before delivering.
**Why it fails:** I filed F001 under "covers" because that is where he caught it, then looked only at the cover. A correction about a sentence shape governs every sentence.
**Check:** scan all headlines and CTA lines.

## F004. 16th September 2026. The CTA is a benefit to the viewer, not an offer of my thing

**What I wrote:** "I've got a starter version, the blank one I build every client's first file from. It's yours. Comment KEEP and I'll send it over."
**What he said:** "don't write CTA like you've done here. you could very easily frame it as a benefit for the viewer: after they've gotten this value, after that they need this more than anything, so comment this and I'll send this. Not like this... the next line also feels corporate."
**RULE:** The CTA answers "what does this do for me?". The carousel has just created a specific need: name it as the reader's, say the thing removes it, then ask. Structure: the need the carousel just created, the benefit in their life tonight, one plain word. Cut corporate tells: "starter version", "template", "resource", "framework" as catalogue nouns, "the one I build every client's first file from" (credential-smuggling), "It's yours.", "I'll send it over." Say the benefit in fifth-grade words, in the reader's life: "fill it in once and you never explain your business to Claude again."
**Why it fails:** it describes the object, not what it does.
**Check:** underline the nouns in the CTA. If it names a product category before a benefit, rewrite.
**Library evidence:** 113, 81, 109, 89 (see `CTA Guide.md`). Keep the friction fix, "(follow me first or it lands in your requests)"; the references use it.

## F005. 12th September 2026. Prose chopped into slides reads waxy; compose the shapes first

**What I wrote:** two rounds of carousel copy as an argument chopped into slides.
**What he said:** "waxy and soulless". He then showed two carousels he considers alive (a warm first-person "shoot your shot" set and a black serif "3 Levels of Branding" set) and asked for a real diagnosis.
**RULE:** The unit of a carousel is the slide shape, not the sentence. Compose the sequence of shapes first (cover, orientation, map, named concept, behaviour bullets, numbered cards, real example, paste-ready asset, nuance, recap, CTA), then fill them. Consecutive slides must not share a shape when the move changes (that is what he calls "dynamic" and "alive"). Give every concept a proper-noun name. At most one quotable line per carousel.
**Why it fails:** writing the argument as prose and then splitting it always reads waxy.
**Check:** list the slide shapes in order before writing; if the list reads like paragraphs of one essay, redo it.
**Library evidence:** 73 ("3 Levels of Branding"), 115 ("How to Shoot Your Shot").

## F006. 12th September 2026. Density was never the problem, abstraction was

**What I wrote:** compressed a 520-word draft to 210 and called it an improvement.
**What he said (diagnosis, in session):** the draft read dead at six-word fragments; conversational words read fast at any length.
**RULE:** Do not compress to fix a dead draft. Fix abstraction: replace named categories of problem ("lack of clarity") with behaviours the reader catches themselves doing ("you add 'just following up, sorry to bother you'"). Soul has two routes: a narrator with real stakes and permission to be uncool (needs HIS stories, so ask, never invent) or relentless behavioural second person.
**Check:** is each slide a behaviour, a scene or a quote, or is it a category noun?

## F007. 12th September 2026. Give the promised asset on the slide; gate only the assembly

**What he said:** covers understandable at a glance, every concept given a proper-noun name, and "the promised asset given away on the slide rather than gated".
**RULE:** When the cover promises a thing (the prompt, the list, the script), deliver it on the slides. Gate only the extended or assembled version behind the comment word (R46). Tension note: this and the CTA gap rule do not conflict; deliver the promise, hold back the assembly.
**Check:** reread the cover promise; is every promised item on a slide?

## F008. 10th October 2026. Cover options: aim at the biggest group, promise what they want most

**What I wrote:** six covers for a motion-graphics idea, each missing in a different way.
**What he said:** the log in `Covers.md` section 6.
**RULE:** Aim at the belief, objection, fear or wish held by the largest group who will see the post. Promise what they want most (quality with less effort), not a side benefit (speed). An authority must be a real known name. Never write the words "unpopular opinion". Keep a thought experiment short. A reverse-advice outcome must be something they genuinely hate.
**Check:** name the bet each cover makes about the viewer, and the size of the group.

## F009. 10th October 2026. A 9-slide, roughly 30-words-a-slide draft was "way too short"

**What I wrote:** "Brands want creators the audience trusts": 9 slides, 15 to 35 words each.
**What he said:** "make it a bit more detailed coz the previous one was kept way too short."
**RULE:** When the idea has real explanation, write 10 to 12 slides and let explaining slides run 45 to 80 words. The library's median body slide (36 words) is a floor for slides that carry a reason, an example or a story, not a target. Add the missing moves (the objection, the "how", the test), not filler. See R15.
**Why it fails:** I treated the swipe file's median as a ceiling and hid the substance behind short lines.
**Check:** for each explaining slide, does it carry a reason or an example? If a slide only labels, add the reason.
**Library evidence:** 75, 99, 110, 111 (long slides with a reason); 12-slide posts such as 86, 73, 100, 102.

## F010. 11th October 2026. Advice anyone could give is cut; teach the author's own method

**What I wrote:** a how-to of generic tips (give a reference, write your taste down, finish one screen, name what's wrong) plus mistakes to avoid.
**What he said:** "It feels generic and I feel like this advice can be given by anyone so I don't want to give this advice in this carousel. The value inside of it really needs to help them make cool apps quickly to make there life easier (in terms of work done and time saved) just like this app saves me time money and effort of making carousel."
**RULE:** Before keeping a tip, ask whether a stranger with no experience of the author could have written it. If yes, cut it. A how-to teaches the author's own build as the worked example (before and after, what it saves) and gives the reader steps that get them a working result fast.
**Why it fails:** tips about good practice carry no proof and no result, so the reader gets nothing they could not find anywhere, and the author's experience is wasted.
**Check:** for every slide, is the proof the author's own build and does the reader leave with a faster way to a result? Is there a before and after of work saved?
**Library evidence:** 106 (his exact stack and costs), 05 and 07 (the build shown working), 04.
