# Rules

The laws of this writer. Each one says what to do, why it holds (the evidence, with post numbers from the library), and how to check it in a draft. They came from 116 swiped carousels, 16 Tinna Loaiza posts with measured comment rates, the earlier Writer vault's craft laws, and every correction the original author gave.

**Read the first rule again before you use any other.**

## R0. None of this is a template

These are patterns observed in posts that worked, with the reasoning attached. If a note could be applied mechanically to any topic, it is wrong. What is being learned is why this line came after that line. A rule says what a reader needs at that point; it never hands you the sentence. Where a rule and the idea disagree, the idea wins. Where a number and the idea disagree, the idea wins.

Feedback stacks. Nothing here is ever replaced, only added to. Fixing the newest note while quietly dropping an older one is itself the failure, and it is the most repeated failure on record. Rules from `Feedback Log.md` sit on top of everything in this file.

## A. Truth and proof

**R1. Never invent facts about the author.** No fabricated results, numbers, stories, testimonials, clients, quotes or dates. Where the post needs one and the author has not given it, write `[ADD: real number or story here]` in the draft and list it under "Needs you" after the copy. The one exception is a cover headline, where a real-shaped number may be written straight in so the cover can be judged; flag it as a stand-in.
*Check:* every number, name and quote in the draft either came from the user, came from a named real source, or carries an ADD marker.

**R2. Use their words verbatim.** When the user tells you what they said, write it that way, in their words. A tighter rewrite of a pitch that never happened is a fabrication wearing better clothes. Every detail he supplies is either used or deliberately cut; dropping detail to keep slides short is usually the wrong trade, because detail is what turns a summary into a story.

**R3. Every claim is backed straight away.** A claim followed by another claim is not backing. Back it on the same slide or the next one with one of: the author's own number, a named borrowed authority or book, a screenshot or visible receipt, a quoted line someone said, a worked example, an analogy that genuinely bridges, a logical reason ("because…"), or open humour. Measured: about 82% of the strong-claim slides in the library are backed within a slide, and 70% of body slides carry some evidence (a number, a quote, an example or a "because"). The real figure is higher, because borrowed proof and logic carry no digits.
*Evidence:* 102 opens on `$896K+`; 75's turn is `0.13%` against a healthy `0.4% to 0.8%`; 86 shows the actual client email; 95 shows the good and the bad prompt side by side; 110 gives the reason a skill is kept (read first, then summarise).
*Check:* underline each claim in the draft. If the next 40 words contain no number, name, quote, example or because, it is unbacked.

**R4. Big numbers need visible support, and absolutes need explanation.** Post 42's "Opportunity score: 22/100", 48's "73,172 people were stuck", 40's "Thinking is forgettable" and 50's "I guarantee" are persuasive but unsupported. Do not learn certainty as a virtue. A number nobody can source is worse than none.

**R5. Never claim to know what strangers thought, saw or did.** "Every freelancer scrolled past that post" is unknowable and reads as arrogance. State what you noticed, what happened to you, or what the reader likely feels in their own words.

**R6. Honour the promise on the cover.** A cover number the body does not deliver (14 promises ten and delivers three; 85's "6-week roadmap" runs to week 8; 78 promises five videos and gives three; 49 promises 21 days and sells the protocol) is the commonest self-inflicted wound. If the cover says 7, there are 7. If it says "in 3 minutes", the method is under 3 minutes.

**R7. Proof arrives early.** Slide 2 buys trust and it does not move. After a big claim the reader is skeptical, not curious. 42% of the later posts carry a proof number on the cover, slide 2 or slide 3 (35% over all posts), and 69% of covers in the later set carry some digit. Put the receipt on slide 2 or 3: the number, the screenshot, the named source, the short story behind the conclusion, or visible effort.

## B. Job, genre and shape

**R8. Decide the job before writing a word.** Every post is built for reach or for leads, never both at once (Tinna Loaiza's whole dataset). Reach posts strip out anything that reads as marketing and accept no CTA or a soft follow; lead posts accept a lower like ceiling in exchange for a comment spike. The default goal in this system is views first, then leads, so use a CTA unless the close is the point.

**R9. Pick the genre, then the structure.** Six genres obey different rules: Argument (a claim defended), Inventory (parallel items), Build (dependent steps), Story (events whose meaning changes), Curation (existing work interpreted), Named model (a ladder or trio). Library mix: Build 29%, Argument 28%, Inventory 25%, Story 6%, Curation 5%, Model 3%, Offer 2%. Boundaries are purposes, not boxes; hybrids are fine. Choose by what the reader can do on the last slide (see `Formats and Slide Shapes.md`).

**R10. Length follows the work the reader must do, never a shape.** The median is 9 slides; 47% sit at 7 to 9, 30% at 10 to 12, 13% at 6 or fewer, 9% at 13 or more. Count the distinct moves, and that is the slide count. A cover and a last slide are always their own slides. A 15-slide post where every slide is one sentence reads faster than a dense 6-slide one, so words per slide, not slide count, sets the load. The old 4 to 9 rule in the Writer vault is superseded by this.

**R11. One dominant job per slide.** Post 12's twelve slides work because no slide carries two steps of the argument. The exception is a move that answers itself in place (42 puts the rejection and its replacement on one slide because the replacement answers the rejection).

**R12. Every slide stands alone and hands over.** Read in isolation it still makes one point; its last line leaves a doubt, an unfinished contrast, a label or a promise that the next slide pays. If a slide only works as the second sentence of a paragraph, it is prose, not a slide.

**R13. Compose the shapes first, then fill them.** Writing the argument as prose and chopping it into slides always reads waxy. Plan the sequence of slide shapes (cover, frame, named concept, behaviour bullets, numbered cards, real example, paste-ready asset, nuance, recap, CTA), then fill each. This is the user's own diagnosis from 12th September 2026.

**R14. The texture rule: vary when the move changes, hold when the slides are parallel.** Nine identical slides worked for post 03 (2.1K comments on 518 likes); breaking the frame inside a list makes the list look improvised. But when each slide makes a different move, never let two neighbours share a silhouette. A post needs at least one single-line slide, one paragraph and one list unless the genre is a pure parallel set. Texture inside a held frame comes from which tiers are present (30), changing evidence (55, 56, 47) or escalating scale.

**R15. Depth is a requirement, not a risk.** the user called a 9-slide, roughly 30-words-a-slide carousel "way too short" and asked for more detail. The library's median body slide is 36 words and its ceiling for a normal slide is about 60, so those are floors for explaining slides, not targets: slides that carry a reason, an example or a story run 45 to 80 words, and a post with real explanation runs 10 to 12 slides. Cut padding, never substance. Conversational words read fast at any length; telegraphic fragments read dead at six. Density was never the problem, abstraction was.

## C. The cover (full detail in `Covers.md`)

**R16. Plain beats clever.** The cover names its subject in plain words, so a stranger can finish "this is about…" in two seconds. A riddle, an artefact or an image the reader has to decode is confusion, not curiosity. Hide the method, never the subject. Default to a plain benefit line (number or limit, what it is, the result). Save the clever line for the thesis slide where it has earned its context.

**R17. Never write a negation followed by a correction, anywhere.** "You're not X, you're Y", "It's not X, it's Y", "You don't have an X problem, you have a Y problem", "The problem isn't X, it's Y." This is a line-level rule for every sentence in the carousel, not a cover rule. Scan every headline before delivering. (Feedback entries F001, F003.) The swipe file does contain the pattern (09's cover, Tinna Loaiza's default teaching device is "Not X. It's Y."). Those are records of what others did. the user's ban wins, and his voice rules ban it too.

**R18. The number is a credential or a limit.** 40% of all covers carry a digit (29% of the first 71, 57% of the later posts). The digit is increasingly proof or a boundary: `$64/month`, `1000 hours`, `<3 minutes`, `$896K+`, `0 to 5 clients`. Never a list count that the body does not honour (R6).

**R19. Aim the cover at the belief, objection, fear or wish held by the largest group who will see it.** A perfect belief shift that only a handful hold misses. Find the biggest one among the biggest group.

**R20. A hedge de-risks a promise.** "(almost) Anything", "hardly anyone", a colon instead of a claim. A flat superlative reads as a marketer; a qualifier lets the promise survive scrutiny (Tinna).

**R21. A concrete provocation owes its explanation on the next slide.** A cover that insults, accuses or threatens must be followed by the reason (52's "Five years ago, all of these gave you an edge", 60 before the legitimacy argument). Provoking without paying is a rant.

## D. Flow and belief

**R22. The question chain is the structure.** Every slide creates a question in the reader's head and the next slide answers it. If a slide answers a question nobody asked, it is filler; if a question goes unanswered for two slides, they have left. Walk it before writing: slide 1 says X, what does the reader now want to know, slide 2 answers exactly that, what does that raise. The explicit link is one route (09's orange forward lines, 04's dangling objections, 75's "Then…/But…" chain); a dependency in the words is another (44's repeated "You don't need"). 29% of posts use forward links on two or more slides.

**R23. Slide 2 pays the debt slide 1 left.** Work out which of New, Easy, Safe and Big the cover already spent, and deliver the missing ones immediately. A big claim followed by more bigness reads as bragging. To deliver Safe: step into their awareness bubble and say the doubt they are thinking, then make the result copyable ("both of these are things you can do too"). Slide 2's four jobs in the winners are proof, the old belief or enemy, the frame, or the fix said first. Never restate the cover.

**R24. Name the assumption in their real words, validate it, then pivot.** Say what they already think is coming and refuse it. Agree with the decision they made and name where the belief came from, then move them. Never make them feel stupid for what they did. Their objection appears in quote marks and gets answered (77% of the later posts quote the reader or an enemy line back).

**R25. A belief shift needs four moves in order.** The reader's old belief in their own words; why it is reasonable to hold it; the break (an example, a number or a scene that cannot be explained by the old belief); a replacement belief they can state. Post 63 refuses the quality objection exactly when it could defeat the diagnosis; 22 intercepts the misreading in the same breath ("The customer is not unimpressed. They are unaware."); 100 separates meaning from obligation.

**R26. Give the belief-shifting line its own slide, unless the next sentence is what justifies it.** Burying the best line in a paragraph is the most common way to waste it. But 42's redefinition and 52's "Good became the baseline" need the next sentence beside them. Test: does the slide still make sense with the line alone? Alone if yes, joined if the explanation is what proves it.

**R27. The insight lands as a scene or an analogy, never an abstract noun.** A named category of problem ("lack of clarity") is the failure; the behaviour the reader catches themselves doing is the cure ("you add 'just following up, sorry to bother you'").

**R28. Remove the next excuse.** After the main idea, answer the objection that would stop the thumb: "You don't need 50 pages." "Don't jump from 30k to 100k. Go 30k, 50k, 75k." "None of it worked because of just a prompt" (75).

**R29. The insight usually lands mid-post, but it lands where its objection is.** Common locations: slide 6 of 12 (01), the middle (15, 19), slide 3 of 7 (50), slide 8 of 11 (53), slide 9 (66). The meaning is earned by withholding it (11: eight slides of story, then one of meaning). Place it where the reader's doubt would otherwise end the read.

**R30. Show the upgrade; do not describe it.** The raw input, what they would normally write, what it becomes. Then a second example so it transfers. A worked example is where the reader believes it (86's client email, 95's prompts, 99's maths, 69's RAOS example).

## E. Format on the slide (full numbers in `Formats and Slide Shapes.md`)

**R31. Pick the shape by the job of the slide.** Name a concept or step: a label or short heading plus 1 to 2 lines. Make a claim with a reason: a bold claim then 2 to 3 plain sentences (the dominant shape). Give parallel actions: 3 to 5 bullets, verb first, under 8 words. Explain why with a story: one paragraph of 40 to 80 words, bold on the two or three words that carry it. Deliver a punch or a pivot: one short line alone. Compare: two labelled blocks. Teach a rule with an example: one repeated internal frame (84's WHAT, WHY, HOW, EXAMPLE). Show what the reader would paste or say: a quoted script set apart.

**R32. Headings are optional when labels do the work.** 59% of body slides carry a bold heading or a label in the whole library, but 71% in the first 71 posts and 45% in the later ones, because `01`, `STEP 3`, `Level 2` and `Problem 4` took over. A heading is a main heading of about 4 to 5 words. Use one when a slide introduces a concept; skip it when the sentence itself carries the argument ("It's presence", 72).

**R33. Bullets are for parallel things only.** 3 to 5 items, same grammar, each readable alone, about 6 words each (71% are under 8). Never bullet a chain of reasoning; reasoning needs "because". The second bullet is where the life goes: mechanism, then their Tuesday (10).

**R34. A paragraph earns its place with a reason or a story.** About 21 words a line, about 2 sentences, one thought, bold on the phrase a skimmer needs. A slide of 100+ words needs a reason: a story the reader is invested in (75), an argument with a stake (110), a post that is the whole read (111). Otherwise cut or split.

**R35. One bolded idea per slide is a better limit than one bolded phrase per line.** Bold is for the thing a skimmer must keep.

**R36. A frame can repeat when the set is parallel.** A fixed internal frame on every slide makes items comparable (61's description, pros, cons, price, user; 67's labelled comparison; 81's title, tagline and three bullets; 96's Problem 1…5, bold claim, reason).

## F. Voice and lines (his voice rules, read `Voice.md`)

**R37. Write it the way it would be said out loud.** Flowing sentences with commas in them, contractions always, sentences of uneven length, fewer full stops. The failure mode is copywriter staccato: chopped fragments with a full stop after every three words. Fragments are allowed only when they have a speaker or a job (51's goose, 53's "I became one. Got the job. Ticked every box.", 49's "Zero."). If it sounds like an advert, it is wrong; if it sounds like a person, it is right.

**R38. Take a side, in one person's voice.** Direct address on 57% of slides, first person on 41%. Quote the enemy and the reader. The best sentences are specific and a little unprofessional; a specific jab beats a polished line. At least one laugh, never aimed at the reader. Humour needs a consistent target (51's inner critic, 55's anxious operator).

**R39. Stay humble, once, on purpose.** One line that doubts or corrects the writer ("I am no Claude expert", "I used to be terrible at this", "I'm lacking here myself") placed mid-deck makes the rest believable (Tinna: self-undercut once per carousel, always the biggest trust purchase). No superiority tax: credit luck where luck was involved, keep the reader on your side of the table, never sneer at the obvious answer.

**R40. Contrast does the arguing, but parallelism alone is not the finding.** 38% of slides carry a negation or contrast. The paired-verdict line ("Liked gets ignored. Wanted gets chosen.") works only when a demonstrated distinction sits behind it. His voice bans neat contrasting pairs used as a formula and the punch of three; use a paired verdict at most once per post, only when the evidence earns it.

**R41. Be specific about their mess.** "20 saved AI tools, 10 browser tabs open, 5 paid subscriptions" (34): nobody has those exact numbers, everyone recognises the shape. Name the feeling under the behaviour (13). Be the witness, not the hero (11).

**R42. Banned in slide copy:** em dashes, semicolons, "not just X but Y", hedges that carry no real doubt, "unlock", "leverage", "delve", "tapestry", "game-changer", "in today's fast-paced world", openers like "So" and "Basically", closing summaries that restate. Reformat a recap; never repeat it.

**R43. Loose is allowed when the person is loose.** Swearing or slang, all-lowercase and typos appear in the winners (102, 93, 96, 106; Tinna leaves typos in) but only where the voice is already like that. the user's voice decides, never "edgy".

## G. The ask (full detail in `CTA Guide.md`)

**R44. The CTA answers "what does this do for me?".** The carousel has just created a specific need; name it, say the thing removes it, then ask. Structure: the need the carousel created, the benefit in their life tonight, the ask in one plain word. (F004.)

**R45. The keyword is the reader's own conclusion.** Typing the word is agreement, not compliance: BASE (113), LADDER (109), LEVEL (73), HABITS (82), REDDIT (94), WISDOM, ARC, STORYLINE (Tinna). Seed it one slide before the ask. Tinna's data: comment rates reach 8% to 16% when the word is seeded, against low single digits for a generic "comment X for the link".

**R46. Teach generously, withhold once, and only the assembly.** Give the parts and the ideas; hold back the sequencing, the full prompt, the real examples, the judgment about which to pick. Manufacture the final gap fresh in the last one or two slides. A carousel that answers everything gets read and saved; one that answers everything except one named thing gets commented on. Tinna's comparison: her best-argued deck on consistency, with nothing withheld, drew 65 comments; two storytelling decks that each withheld one named item (20 prompts, 20 story angles) drew about 1,000 each.

**R47. Name the deliverable and its form.** "my brand new video lesson revealing how to build a personal brand from scratch" (113), "22 MIN FULL GUIDE" (81), "2000+ handpicked viral hooks" (87). Never "a guide", "a resource", "a template". Add a number or a deadline only when the offer is real, never invented scarcity.

**R48. One ask, on the last slide.** 93% of comment CTAs sit on the last slide and 26% also appear earlier as a second ask. Two competing asks weaken both, except a repeated keyword that names the same offer. Recycle one word across posts for one asset; never across assets.

**R49. A strong ending and a CTA are different things.** Posts 36, 47, 51 and 56 end on the idea (19% of the library has no CTA). When the argument is "less screen, lower demands", another platform task would undo it. Choose no CTA deliberately, and say so in the notes.

**R50. Slides lecture, captions confide.** Slides run declarative and second person; the first-person receipts, confessions and hard numbers belong in the caption (Tinna). Disclose first if you ask the reader to disclose.

## H. Faults the library itself warns against

A headline number the body does not honour (14). A cover image unrelated to the argument (14). Stating a principle you do not follow (14 to 17 teach CTA discipline and end on "comment 🔥"). A promise the contents contradict (02). A CTA that describes the author's activity (30). A ranking its own comparison qualifies (61). A deadline the teaching never fulfils (48, 49, 64). A build instruction mistaken for a finished system (69). Do not reconstruct missing steps when supplied numbering has gaps (86, 92). Learn the decision behind an example, not the bracketed slots printed beside it (39, 46).
