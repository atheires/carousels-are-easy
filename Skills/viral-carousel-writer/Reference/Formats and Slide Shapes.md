# Formats and Slide Shapes

The decisions made on every carousel, with the evidence. Numbers are from `Numbers.md` (116 posts). Each decision is a question to answer for this idea, with what winners did when they answered it. None of it is a template.

## 0. A carousel is not an article chopped into slides

An article lets the reader scroll back, skim and re-read. A carousel gives one slide and one decision: swipe or leave. So every slide does three things.

1. **Stands alone.** With no other slide in view it still makes one point (101: one aphorism per slide; 87: one word pair per slide).
2. **Hands over to the next.** It ends on a doubt, an unfinished contrast, a label or a promise (113: "Here's what I mean by that…"; 79: "go back & read this again").
3. **Is shaped for the thumb.** A heading or label says what the slide is before they read it. The body is what they read once they have decided to stay.

## 1. Choose the format before the first line

Ask: *what does the reader get to do when they reach the last slide?* The answer names the format.

| The reader leaves able to… | Format | Seen in | Shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| Do a thing, in order | Step-by-step | 85, 86, 94, 95, 98, 104 | Numbered or `STEP N` label, an action heading, 2 to 5 bullets, one screenshot or example per step |
| Apply a set of independent moves | Tips or rules list | 81, 84, 92, 107, 108 | One item per slide, same internal shape every time, a bold label plus a reason |
| See their situation through a new frame | Named framework or levels | 73, 87, 97, 100, 109 | A named ladder or trio, one slide per level, a recap slide showing all levels together |
| Change a belief | Perspective or belief shift | 72, 88, 93, 99, 101, 113 | A claim, the old belief in quotes, the break, a replacement belief |
| Trust a person enough to buy | Story or case study | 75, 83, 89, 116 | A real number first, what went wrong, what changed, what it produced, the offer |
| Pick the right tool or source | Curation or tool list | 78, 105, 106 | A name, a price or tag, one reason, a ranked order |
| Take a decision about their own life | Lessons list | 82, 103, 112 | A bold maxim, then 2 to 3 sentences of permission |

If the idea fits two rows, the one the CTA sells wins. Library mix: Build 29%, Argument 28%, Inventory 25%, Story 6%, Curation 5%, Model 3%, Offer 2%. The later posts shifted from Argument (34% to 20%) towards Build (27% to 33%) and Named model (0% to 9%).

**Story posts almost never stand alone.** 75, 83 and 89 each end in an offer. A story that earns trust is nearly always the lead-in to a sale.

## 2. Slide count

| Count | Share of posts | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| 6 or fewer | 13% | Tight definition lists, or every slide is a step (87, 98, 104, 65, 29) |
| **7 to 9** | **47%** | **The default** |
| 10 to 12 | 30% | Each item needs a screenshot, an example or a script (86, 73, 100, 102). Rising: 25% of the first 71, 38% of the later set |
| 13 or more | 9% | Only when every slide is one short line (30, 31, 32) or the list is the whole product (81) |

Median 9, mean 9.2. Never pad to a number. Count the distinct moves; that is the slide count. The cover and the CTA are always their own slides.

Some length belongs to promotion, not explanation (67 reaches its point on slide 8 then spends three frames on another post). Count only explanation when sizing.

## 3. Words per slide

| Measure | Value |
|---|---|
| Median body slide | 36 words (mean 44) |
| Typical band (16 to 60 words) | about 69% of body slides |
| 15 words or fewer | 13% |
| 61 to 100 words | 13% |
| Over 100 words | 5% |
| Cover (all) | median 12.5 words |
| Slide 2 | mean 43 words |
| Last slide | mean 30 words |
| Posts averaging 20 or fewer words a slide | 12% |
| 21 to 40 | 39% |
| 41 to 60 | 29% |
| Over 60 | 20% |

**Read this carefully.** 36 is a median, not a target. A slide that carries a reason, an example or a story runs 45 to 80 words. A punch, a pivot or a label runs under 15. The author judged a 30-words-a-slide draft "way too short" for an idea with real explanation. Density was never the problem, abstraction was.

Word economy: about 45% of sentences are 8 words or fewer, the average sentence is 12 words. Mix them: a longer sentence that carries a reason, then a short one that lands the point. Cut openers ("So", "Basically"), restated headings, hedges that carry no real doubt and adjectives that cannot be checked. Keep the concrete noun, the number, the example, the quoted line.

## 4. The second slide

Winners spend slide 2 on one of four jobs, chosen by what the cover left unproven. Never restate the cover.

- **Proof.** A credential, a number, a screenshot: "$896K+ as an artist working with brands like BMW" (102); "328,000 people saw my content in 30 days. Only 0.13% followed" (75).
- **The enemy or the old belief.** "Most 9-5ers build content backwards. They open Instagram…" (113).
- **The frame.** "Every brand sits at one of three levels." (73) / "THERE ARE 6 LEVELS OF STORYTELLING" (109).
- **The fix, said first.** "Stop blaming AI for generic results. It's time to change how you use it." (95).

It also pays the NESB debt (Rules R23): whichever of New, Easy, Safe and Big the cover did not spend.

## 5. Per slide: heading, paragraph, bullets or one-liner

Pick by the job of the slide, not by look.

| Slide job | Shape | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Name a concept or step | A label or short bold heading, then 1 to 2 lines | 73, 98, 109: "STEP 2 Create Design Tokens" |
| Make a claim with a reason | **Bold claim, then 2 to 3 plain sentences** | 103, 113, 82; the dominant shape |
| Give parallel actions or contents | **3 to 5 bullets, each under 8 words, verb first** | 81, 85, 98, 108 |
| Explain why, with a story | One paragraph of 40 to 80 words, bold on the two or three words that carry it | 110, 75, 99 |
| Deliver a punch or a pivot | One short line alone | 72 ("It's presence."), 83 ("Stop planning. Start posting."), 32 and 33 ("Here's the better move:") |
| Compare two things | Two labelled blocks on one slide | 67, 61, 97 |
| Teach a rule with an example | A fixed internal frame repeated on every slide | 84: WHAT, WHY, HOW, EXAMPLE |
| Show what the reader would paste or say | A quoted script or prompt, set apart | 86 (the client email), 95 (good and bad prompt), 104 (the search string) |
| Close with a map | A recap that reformats | 73 slide 11, 102 slide 9, 09, 15, 16, 18 |

### What the measurements say about each element

| Element | Share of body slides | Sizes |
|---|---|---|
| Paragraph | 48% (41% in the first 71, 56% in the later) | A line averages 21 words (median 19), about 2 sentences |
| Bullets | 19% (14% to 25%) | A bullet averages 6.3 words, 71% are under 8; a bullet slide averages 3.4 bullets, 57% hold 3 to 5 |
| Bold heading or label | 59% (71% to 45%) | A heading averages 4.8 words (median 4) |
| Heading plus a short line under it | 22% | |
| Short lines only (no paragraph, no bullets) | 38% (47% to 27%) | |
| Single-line slide | 6% (8% to 4%) | |

By post: paragraphs on at least half the slides, 54% (45% to 67%). Bullets on 40% or more of slides, 21% (17% to 27%). No heading or label anywhere, 23% (8% to 44%). Slides labelled `01`, `Step 2`, `Level 3`, `#4`: 31% (22% to 44%).

**Where they sit:** heading on slide 2 55%, middle 60%, second to last 63%, last slide 53%. Bullets cluster in the middle (19%) and almost never on the last slide (7%). Paragraphs run 46% on slide 2, 48% in the middle, 51% second to last, 34% on the last.

### Rules of thumb that held up

- **Headings are optional when slides carry numbers or labels.** The later set dropped bold headlines because `01`, `STEP 3`, `Problem 4` and `Level 2` do the work.
- **Bullets are for parallel things only.** Three to five items, same grammar, each readable alone. Never bullet a chain of reasoning; reasoning needs "because".
- **A paragraph earns its place when it holds a reason or a story.** One thought, bold on the phrase a skimmer needs.
- **Do not alternate shapes for the sake of variety when the slides are parallel items.** Vary shape only when the move changes (Rules R14).
- **A slide of 100+ words** needs a reason: a story the reader is invested in (75), an argument with a stake (110), or a post that is intentionally the whole read (111). Otherwise cut or split.
- **One bolded idea per slide** is a better limit than one bolded phrase per line.
- **Two tiers separate retrieval from explanation.** A large assertion, a small elaboration; the big lines alone carry the argument (11, 12, 30, 31). Not compulsory: 36 uses one tier.
- **Bullets on the last slide are rare.** The close is a sentence, a recap or the ask.

## 6. How a cold viewer becomes an active reader

The viewer has no reason to be here and one thumb. What moves them from scrolling to reading to acting:

1. **The cover earns the stop.** Plain subject, a stake the biggest group feels, a number as proof or limit, and a short kicker that makes it feel quick.
2. **Slide 2 spends their doubt.** Proof, the old belief in their words, the frame, or the fix. They decide in the first two slides whether this is worth a read.
3. **Each slide hands over a question.** The link is explicit or dependent. If they are asking the next question in their own head, they are reading.
4. **A label or number shows how much is left.** `01` to `07`, `Step 3 of 6`, a level ladder. A finish line makes the middle feel cheap.
5. **A reward lands mid-post.** The named concept, the worked example, the line that changes the belief. This is why the insight sits mid-post.
6. **The ask is the obvious next move.** By the end the reader has a need the carousel created; the comment word is their own conclusion.

## 7. How a thought becomes a viral carousel

The order the best posts use. It is not a template; it is the order a cold reader needs their questions answered.

1. **Who is this for, and what do they secretly believe?** Write the belief as a sentence they would say aloud ("I don't have time", "I'm not creative enough", 77).
2. **What is the real problem underneath?** 77: "Content isn't about you. It's about them." 75: "I did not have a reach problem. I had a 'nobody who sees this knows why they should stay' problem." The second name for the problem is the insight.
3. **Name it.** A short proper-noun concept ("Content GPS", "Mirror, Window, Door", "Emotional Delta") gives the reader something to repeat. 25% of the library names a framework, system or ladder (34% of the later posts).
4. **Show it working once, in full.** One complete worked example with real numbers or a real quoted line (86's email, 95's prompt, 99's maths). The example is where the reader believes it.
5. **Remove the next excuse.** "You don't need 50 pages." The post that answers the next objection stops the thumb leaving.
6. **Close the loop and name the next move.** A recap that reformats, a verdict line, or a single action, then the ask.

Before step 1, decide the job (reach or leads, R8) and the genre.

## 8. Generic AI versus a writer trained on this library

| Situation | An untrained AI | This writer |
|---|---|---|
| Cover | "5 Tips to Grow on Instagram" | Works out the post's job, names a number that is a credential or a limit, makes the stake plain |
| Slide 2 | Restates the cover | Proof, the old belief, the frame or the fix |
| Structure | One essay split every 40 words | Chooses a format first, then a shape per slide |
| Headings | A bold heading on every slide in one style | Numbers or labels carry structure; a heading appears when a slide introduces a concept |
| Bullets | Bullets for everything, each a vague noun phrase | Only for 3 to 5 parallel verb-led actions under 8 words |
| Paragraphs | Four sentences of general advice | One thought, a reason, bold on the phrase that matters |
| Examples | "For example, a fitness coach could…" | A real quoted line, number or screenshot from the client |
| Voice | Corporate, hedged, balanced | One person, direct, quotes the reader, takes a side |
| Insight | A list of tips the reader already knows | A named concept or a second name for the problem |
| Ending | "Follow for more!" | A recap that restates the system in a new shape, then a named deliverable and a word tied to the content |
| CTA | "Comment 'guide' for the link" | The keyword is the reader's own conclusion; the deliverable is named with its form |
| Honesty | Invents stats and quotes | Asks, or writes ADD markers |
| Rhythm | Every sentence the same length | A longer reason, then a short verdict |
| Closing | Summarises what was already said | Reformats it, or sets the reader's next move |
